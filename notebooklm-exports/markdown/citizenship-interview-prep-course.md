### The Big Picture: One Officer, One Conversation

The naturalization interview is a single, mostly friendly conversation with one USCIS officer. It is not an interrogation and there are no trick questions. The officer has your entire file — your Form N-400, your green card record, your travel history — open in front of them, and their job is to confirm three things: that you are who you say you are, that everything on your application is true and up to date, and that you meet the requirements for citizenship (including the English and civics tests). For most well-prepared applicants the whole thing is over in **15 to 30 minutes**.

Your Interview

Yours will be at the **USCIS San Jose Field Office**. You’ll each have your own separate interview with your own officer — spouses are interviewed one at a time, not together. That’s normal. Bring everything on the Module 14 checklist for each of you.

### Arrival & Check-In

Plan to **arrive about 30 minutes early**. You’ll go through airport-style security at the entrance (no weapons, and it’s smart to leave anything sharp in the car). Then:

-   Check in at the front desk or a self-check-in kiosk and show your **appointment/interview notice** (Form I-797C) and a photo ID.
-   Take a seat in the waiting room. Wait times vary — sometimes you’re called right on time, sometimes there’s a delay. Bring patience.
-   An officer will come out and call your name, then walk you back to a private office. They may make small talk on the walk — this is where your English speaking test quietly begins (see Module 3).

### The Oath Before Questioning

The very first thing that happens when you sit down is that the officer asks you to **raise your right hand and swear (or affirm) to tell the truth**. This is a promise that everything you say in the interview is true. Take it seriously: it’s why consistency between your written answers and your spoken answers matters so much. Answer honestly even if you think an honest answer might be unfavorable — a truthful “yes” is almost always better than a false “no” the officer can see through in your file.

> **Warning:** Don’t Confuse the Two Oaths
>
> This truth-telling oath at the *start* of the interview is **not** the Oath of Allegiance. The Oath of Allegiance is the formal ceremony that actually makes you a citizen, and it happens later — sometimes the same day, sometimes at a scheduled ceremony weeks later. (Two of the civics questions, #66 and #67, are about the Pledge and the Oath of Allegiance — see Module 8.)

### What Actually Happens in the Room

Once you’re sworn in, the interview follows a predictable arc:

1.  **Identity & document check.** The officer confirms your name, date of birth, and address, and reviews your green card, passport(s), and state ID.
2.  **N-400 review.** They go through your application, largely in order, asking you to confirm or update answers out loud (Module 2).
3.  **English test.** Woven throughout for speaking, plus a short reading sentence and a short writing sentence (Module 3).
4.  **Civics test.** Up to 20 questions from the 128; you need **12 correct** (Modules 4–11). The officer usually stops as soon as you’ve reached 12 right.
5.  **Wrap-up.** You sign your application (and often your photos), and the officer tells you the decision.

### The Officer’s Role & Demeanor

Officers are professionals doing a routine job dozens of times a day. Most are courteous and businesslike; a few are chatty, a few are quiet. Don’t read anything into their tone — a stern officer is not a bad sign, and a friendly one is not a guarantee. If you don’t understand a question, it is completely fine to say *“Could you please repeat that?”* or *“Could you say that a different way?”* Asking for a repeat does not count against you.

### What Happens After

At the end, the officer gives you **Form N-652, Notice of Examination Results**, which shows one of three outcomes:

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Granted | You passed everything. Best case: you may be scheduled for a same-day oath ceremony, or you’ll get a notice (Form N-445) for a ceremony on a later date. You are not a citizen until you take the Oath of Allegiance. |
| Continued | The officer needs something more — a document you didn’t bring, or a re-test on a portion you didn’t pass. You typically get a second chance within ~60–90 days. This is not a denial. |
| Denied | The application was not approved. This is uncommon for well-prepared, eligible applicants and usually comes with a written explanation and appeal rights. |

> **Tip:** Reassuring Reality
>
> Nationally, the overwhelming majority of applicants pass. If you fail the English or civics portion, you get a **second attempt** (on just the part you failed) — you do not start over. So the pressure on any single question is much lower than it feels.

### The Officer Reads Your Application Back to You

The heart of the interview is the officer walking through your **Form N-400**, largely in the order the form is printed, and asking you to confirm each answer out loud. They are doing two things at once: **verifying the facts** and **testing your English** by making you respond to real questions. The single most important habit is simple: **re-read your own N-400 the night before** so your spoken answers match what you wrote. Officers notice inconsistencies, and an unexplained mismatch is what turns a five-minute review into a longer conversation.

> **Tip:** The Golden Rule
>
> Answer **honestly and consistently**. If something has changed since you filed — a new job, a new trip abroad, a new address, a traffic ticket — just say so; the officer will update the record. Updates are normal and expected. What causes problems is a spoken answer that contradicts your written one with no explanation.

### Sections the Officer Focuses On

#### 1\. Biographic information & eligibility basis

Name, date of birth, address, how long you’ve been a permanent resident, and whether you’re applying under the **5-year rule** or the **3-year rule** (married to and living with a U.S. citizen). They confirm you meet the continuous-residence and physical-presence requirements.

#### 2\. Travel history (trips outside the U.S.)

Expect: *“How many trips have you taken outside the United States in the last five years? Any trip longer than six months?”* Bring a written list of your trips (dates out / dates in). A single trip of **6 months or more** can raise continuous-residence questions, so be ready to explain any long trip. This is exactly why your passports and any travel records are on the document checklist.

#### 3\. Employment & residence history

Where you’ve lived and worked for the past five years. Minor gaps are fine; just be able to account for them.

#### 4\. Family & marital history

Spouse, children, prior marriages. For 3-year (marriage-based) applicants, the officer confirms the marriage is real and ongoing. Know your spouse’s basic details (date of birth, where they were born, current employer).

#### 5\. Good Moral Character — the “Have you EVER…” questions

This is the block of yes/no questions officers watch most closely. They are asked verbatim and quickly, and they cover things like:

-   Have you **ever** been arrested, cited, detained, or convicted of a crime (even if it was dismissed or expunged)?
-   Have you **ever** failed to file a required tax return, or owe any overdue taxes?
-   Have you **ever** been a member of, or associated with, **any organization, association, party, or group** — anywhere in the world?
-   Have you **ever** been a member of the **Communist Party**, a totalitarian party, or a terrorist organization?
-   Have you **ever** advocated the overthrow of any government by force?
-   Have you **ever** persecuted anyone; or been involved in **genocide**, torture, or killing?
-   Have you **ever** lied to any U.S. government official to gain an immigration benefit, or claimed to be a U.S. citizen?
-   Have you **ever** not supported the U.S. Constitution, or not been willing to bear arms / perform noncombatant service / perform work of national importance if the law required it?

> **Warning:** “Any organization” is broader than you think
>
> The membership question means *any* group — a professional association, a religious group, a sports club, a school PTA, a union. It is **not** a trick and being in a normal group is not a problem. The mistake to avoid is answering “no” reflexively when the true answer is “yes.” The concern is only with specific groups (Communist/totalitarian parties, terrorist groups). Answer truthfully; ordinary memberships are fine.

### The New 2026 Trend: Defining Legal Terms

A newer pattern reported at interviews: after asking a moral-character question, the officer asks you to **define the key legal word in it** — to make sure you actually understood the question you just answered, and that your English comprehension is real. You don’t need a law-school definition; a simple, correct one-sentence explanation is plenty. The most commonly asked terms:

| Term | A simple, interview-ready definition |
| --- | --- |
| Perjury | Lying, or telling a lie, after you have sworn or promised to tell the truth (for example, lying under oath). |
| Allegiance | Loyalty and faithfulness to a country. Pledging allegiance to the U.S. means being loyal to the United States. |
| Genocide | The deliberate killing of a large group of people because of their race, religion, ethnicity, or nationality. |
| Persecute / Persecution | To hurt, harm, or oppress people because of their race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a group. |
| Totalitarian | A form of government where one party or ruler has total control and people do not have freedom (for example, a dictatorship). |
| Bear arms | To carry or use weapons — for example, to serve in the military and fight if the country needs you. |
| Noncombatant service | Helping the military without fighting — for example, working as a medic, cook, or in another support role. |
| Good moral character | Being an honest, law-abiding person — obeying the law, paying taxes, telling the truth, and meeting your responsibilities. |

> **Tip:** How to Handle a Definition Request
>
> If the officer asks “What does *perjury* mean?” take a breath and answer in one plain sentence, in your own words. It doesn’t have to be exact — it has to show you understood the question. If you truly don’t know a word, it’s okay to say “I’m not sure of that word — could you explain it?” That’s honest and the officer can rephrase.

#### Flashcards: Legal Terms You May Be Asked to Define

The English requirement has three parts — **speaking, reading, and writing** — and the good news is that the vocabulary is small, fixed, and published in advance. You are tested on civics-themed sentences built from a short, official word list. Master those lists and the reading/writing portions become nearly automatic.

### Part 1 — Speaking (tested throughout)

There is no separate “speaking test.” The officer evaluates your ability to speak and understand ordinary English **during the whole interview** — the small talk on the walk to the office, the identity questions, and especially the N-400 review, where you have to understand each question and respond. You pass by simply **understanding and answering the officer’s everyday questions** about your application.

> **Tip:** Speaking Tips
>
> -   Answer the question that was asked — short, direct answers are perfect. You don’t need long sentences.
> -   It’s fine to ask “*Could you repeat that, please?*” A repeat request is normal and does not lower your score.
> -   Don’t rush. A calm, clear “yes” or “no” beats a fast, mumbled sentence.

### Part 2 — Reading

The officer shows you sentences on a tablet or paper. You must **read 1 out of up to 3 sentences correctly** aloud. You don’t need perfect pronunciation — you need to convey that you understood the sentence, without long pauses or leaving out / substituting words that change the meaning. Every reading sentence is built from this official list:

| Category | Reading vocabulary |
| --- | --- |
| People | Abraham Lincoln, George Washington |
| Civics | American flag, Bill of Rights, capital, citizen, city, Congress, country, Father of Our Country, government, President, right, Senators, state / states, White House |
| Places | America, United States, U.S. |
| Holidays | Presidents’ Day, Memorial Day, Flag Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Columbus Day, Thanksgiving |
| Question words | How, What, When, Where, Who, Why |
| Verbs | can, come, do / does, elects, have / has, is / are / was / be, lives / lived, meet, name, pay, vote, want |
| Other (function) | a, for, here, in, of, on, the, to, we |
| Other (content) | colors, dollar bill, first, largest, many, most, north, one, one hundred / 100, people, second, south |

#### Practice reading sentences

-   Who was the first President?
-   What is the capital of the United States?
-   Where is the White House?
-   Why do people want to come to America?
-   When do we vote for President?
-   Name one right of the people.

### Part 3 — Writing

The officer reads a sentence aloud and you **write 1 out of up to 3 sentences correctly**. Minor spelling slips are usually okay as long as the sentence is understandable and no word is left out or changed into a different word. Every writing sentence uses this official list:

| Category | Writing vocabulary |
| --- | --- |
| People | Adams, Lincoln, Washington |
| Civics | American Indians, capital, citizens, Civil War, Congress, Father of Our Country, flag, free, freedom of speech, President, right, Senators, state / states, White House |
| Places | Alaska, California, Canada, Delaware, Mexico, New York City, United States, Washington, Washington, D.C. |
| Months | February, May, June, July, September, October, November |
| Holidays | Presidents’ Day, Memorial Day, Flag Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Columbus Day, Thanksgiving |
| Verbs | can, come, elect, have / has, is / was / be, lives / lived, meets, pay, vote, want |
| Other (function) | and, during, for, here, in, of, on, the, to, we |
| Other (content) | blue, dollar bill, fifty / 50, first, largest, most, north, one, one hundred / 100, people, red, second, south, taxes, white |

#### Practice writing sentences

-   Washington was the first President.
-   The capital of the United States is Washington, D.C.
-   Citizens can vote.
-   People pay taxes.
-   The flag is red, white, and blue.
-   California has the most people.

> **Tip:** Tips for Non-Native Speakers
>
> -   **Practice out loud, every day.** Read the practice sentences aloud and have each other dictate a writing sentence to write down. Ten minutes a day beats one long session.
> -   **Learn the list, not every sentence.** Since all sentences come from the two word lists, drilling the words themselves covers every possible sentence.
> -   **Capital letters and periods help** but small errors are forgiven — focus on getting the *words* right and not skipping any.
> -   **Listen for the whole sentence** before you start writing. It’s fine to ask the officer to repeat the sentence for the writing test.
> -   **Numbers can be digits or words** (“50” or “fifty”) — either is accepted.

### A Full Sample Interview Script

This is a realistic walk-through. Your actual interview will vary, but the shape is the same.

In the Waiting Room → The Office

**Officer:** “Zeesha Furniturewala?” *(You stand.)* “Hi, I’m Officer Lee. Please follow me. How are you today?”
**You:** “I’m well, thank you. A little nervous!”
**Officer:** “That’s completely normal. Right this way. Please remain standing — raise your right hand. Do you swear that the statements you will give today are true, to the best of your knowledge?”
**You:** “I do.” *(Then sit when invited.)*

Identity & N-400 Review

**Officer:** “May I see your green card, passport, and driver’s license? … Thank you. Please state your full name and date of birth.”
**You:** “My name is Zeesha Furniturewala. My date of birth is \[…\].”
**Officer:** “What is your current address?”
**You:** “\[Street\], Sunnyvale, California, 94087.”
**Officer:** “How long have you been a lawful permanent resident? … Since we last spoke on the form, have you taken any trips outside the United States?”
**You:** “Yes — one trip to visit family, for about two weeks in \[month\].” *(Have your travel list ready.)*
**Officer:** “Have you ever been arrested or detained by any law enforcement officer?”
**You:** “No.” *(Answer honestly; if yes, explain briefly and bring documents.)*
**Officer:** “Have you ever been a member of any group or organization? … What does ‘persecute’ mean?” *(The 2026 define-a-term pattern.)*
**You:** “It means to harm or mistreat someone because of things like their religion, race, or political opinion.”

English: Reading & Writing

**Officer:** “Now please read this sentence out loud.” *(Shows: “Why do people want to come to America?”)*
**You:** *(Read it clearly, no skipped words.)*
**Officer:** “Good. Now please write this sentence: ‘Citizens can vote.’”
**You:** *(Write it, capital C, period at the end.)*

Civics (stops at 12 correct)

**Officer:** “I’m going to ask you some civics questions. What is the supreme law of the land?” → **You:** “The Constitution.”
“How many U.S. senators are there?” → “One hundred.”
“Who is one of your state’s U.S. senators now?” → “Alex Padilla.”
“What is the capital of your state?” → “Sacramento.”
“Why does the flag have 13 stripes?” → “For the 13 original colonies.” *(…and so on, up to 20, stopping at 12 correct.)*

Wrap-Up

**Officer:** “Congratulations — you passed the English and civics tests. Please review and sign here. You’ll receive a notice about your oath ceremony.” *(Hands you Form N-652 marked ‘Granted.’)*
**You:** “Thank you very much!”

### Red Flags to Avoid

> **Warning:** Don’t Do These
>
> -   **Don’t guess or lie** on a “Have you ever…” question. A truthful “yes” with a short explanation is far safer than a false “no.”
> -   **Don’t answer a question you didn’t understand.** Ask the officer to repeat or rephrase — that’s expected, not a penalty.
> -   **Don’t volunteer extra information** beyond what’s asked. Answer the question, then stop.
> -   **Don’t bring your phone out** or take photos; keep it silenced and away.
> -   **Don’t contradict your N-400** without explaining. If something changed, just say so.
> -   **Don’t argue or debate politics.** Answer civics questions factually; opinions aren’t needed.
> -   **Don’t forget documents.** A missing document can turn a “granted” into a “continued.” Use the Module 14 checklist.

Everything comes down to this: bring the right documents, arrive calm and early, and let your preparation carry you. Print or screenshot this checklist and pack the night before — **each of you needs your own full set**.

### 💼 Documents to Bring

Always Bring

-   **Interview appointment notice** (Form I-797C) — the letter that scheduled this interview.
-   **Green card** (Permanent Resident Card) — original.
-   **All passports** — current and expired — plus any travel documents (they show your trips).
-   **State ID / driver’s license.**
-   **A written list of every trip outside the U.S.** in the last 5 years (dates left / dates returned).

> **Tip:** Bring If It Applies to You
>
> -   **Marriage-based (3-year) applicants:** marriage certificate; proof you live together and share a life (lease/mortgage, joint bank statements, joint tax returns, insurance, children’s birth certificates); and evidence of your spouse’s U.S. citizenship.
> -   **Name changes:** marriage certificate, divorce decree, or court order showing the change.
> -   **Prior marriages:** divorce decrees or death certificates ending each earlier marriage (yours and your spouse’s).
> -   **Tax records:** IRS tax return transcripts for the last 3–5 years (especially if asked, or if you ever owed).
> -   **Any arrest/citation:** certified court dispositions and evidence any fines were paid — even for dismissed or expunged cases.
> -   **Selective Service** confirmation (men who lived in the U.S. between ages 18–25).
> -   **Anything specifically listed on your interview notice.**

### 👔 What to Wear

Neat and respectful — business casual is ideal. You don’t need a suit, but avoid shorts, tank tops, slogans, or anything you’d wear to the gym. Dressing like it’s an important appointment helps you feel the part, too.

### ⏰ When to Arrive

-   **Arrive ~30 minutes early** to clear security and check in without rushing.
-   Plan your drive to the **San Jose field office** with traffic buffer; know where to park.
-   Silence your phone and keep it away during the interview.
-   Eat something beforehand and take a breath in the parking lot — you’re ready.

### 🚫 What NOT to Do

-   Don’t bring prohibited items (weapons, and check your notice for other restrictions).
-   Don’t use your phone, record, or take photos inside.
-   Don’t guess on honesty questions, and don’t volunteer more than asked.
-   Don’t leave documents in the car that you might need — bring the whole folder in.
-   Don’t panic over one hard civics question; you have room to miss several and still pass.

### 🎉 After the Interview: The Oath Ceremony

If your result is **Granted**, one last step makes you a citizen: the **Oath of Allegiance** at a naturalization ceremony. This may happen the **same day**, or you’ll get **Form N-445** with a later date. At the ceremony you will:

1.  Check in and **turn in your green card** (you won’t need it anymore).
2.  Answer a few final questions on Form N-445 (anything that changed since the interview).
3.  **Take the Oath of Allegiance** together with other new citizens.
4.  Receive your **Certificate of Naturalization** — you are now a U.S. citizen!

> **Tip:** First Things To Do As Citizens
>
> -   **Register to vote** (often available right at the ceremony).
> -   **Apply for a U.S. passport** using your Certificate of Naturalization.
> -   Update your status with Social Security and your employer if needed.
> -   Keep your Certificate of Naturalization **safe** — store the original securely and use certified copies when possible.
