PHI: the constraint that reframes the job

Why “roll out the coding assistant” is a different question here
By the end of this module you will
  • Know precisely what PHI is, and why it shows up in places engineers do not expect
  • Be able to explain what a BAA does and — more importantly — what it does not do
  • Understand “minimum necessary” and why it is the principle that kills most naive agent designs

The three-sentence version

PHI is individually identifiable health information held or transmitted by a covered entity or business associate. A covered entity is a provider, plan or clearinghouse. A business associate is a vendor that handles PHI on a covered entity's behalf — and a business associate's own vendors are subcontractors who need their own agreements, which is how the chain reaches your AI provider.

The reframe — say this one out loud until it is natural

At a normal software company, the question “should we roll out an AI coding assistant?” is a question about productivity and cost.

At Included Health it is a question about whether a third party is about to become an unauthorised recipient of protected health information — because the repository may contain PHI in test fixtures, the logs almost certainly contain it, the Jira tickets contain it in the reproduction steps, and the engineer debugging a production incident at 2am is going to paste a payload into something.

Same tool. Completely different question. That reframe is the interview.

Where PHI actually hides in an engineering org

The formal identifier list matters less than knowing where it leaks in practice. Ranked by how often engineers are surprised:

SurfaceHow PHI gets thereRisk
Production logsRequest/response bodies logged on error; stack traces carrying patient objects; debug logging left on; correlation IDs joined to member recordsHighest
Jira / ticket bodiesSupport escalations pasted verbatim: member name, DOB, the actual clinical complaint, screenshotsHigh

Analytics / notebooksAd-hoc queries against warehouse tables, cached results in notebook outputs committed to gitHigh
Error tracking (Sentry etc.)Breadcrumbs and local variable capture on exceptionMedium-high
Source codeUsually clean; occasionally a hardcoded example, a fixture, or a comment quoting a real caseMedium

Opinion Note the ordering. The instinct is to worry about source code, because that is what a coding assistant reads. The actual exposure is concentrated in logs and tickets — which is precisely where the role's other two goals (debugging agents, Jira triage) point. The role brief has, without necessarily meaning to, named the two highest-PHI-risk surfaces in the company as target use cases. Noticing that out loud is a strong move.

What a BAA does — and what it does not

A BAA does

Contractually bind the vendor to HIPAA's safeguard, breach-notification and use-limitation requirements.

Make it lawful to disclose PHI to that vendor for permitted purposes.

Flow obligations down to their subcontractors.

Give you contractual recourse and audit rights.

A BAA does not

Cover every product the vendor sells. Coverage is per-service, and often per-feature. This is the single most misunderstood point.

Make the deployment secure. It is paper, not a control.

Relieve you of “minimum necessary.”

Automatically extend to integrations, plugins, MCP servers or third-party tools the vendor connects to.

The sentence that will land in the room

“A signed BAA is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. Coverage is per-feature, not per-vendor — the same vendor will cover their chat API and explicitly exclude their batch endpoint, their file storage, and half their agent surface. So the question is never ‘do we have a BAA with them,’ it is ‘is the specific feature we are about to use on the covered list, in the configuration we are about to use it in.’”

Module 3 shows you three vendors where exactly this distinction bites.

Minimum necessary — the principle that kills naive agent design

HIPAA requires that uses and disclosures of PHI be limited to the minimum necessary to accomplish the purpose. This is the principle that makes broad, ambient AI access architecturally wrong rather than merely risky.

Concretely: an agent that can query the whole logging backend to debug one incident is not minimum-necessary, even with a BAA in place, even if it is technically authorised. An agent scoped to one service's logs, in one time window, with identifiers already tokenised, is a defensible design. Same capability, different blast radius, different conversation with your privacy office. Module 5 builds this out properly.

Two more things worth knowing, stated carefully

Opinion De-identification is a higher bar than engineers think. HIPAA recognises two routes: removing a defined set of identifiers (the “Safe Harbor” method), or a qualified expert determining re-identification risk is very small (the “Expert Determination” method). Regex-scrubbing names out of a log is neither of those, and calling scrubbed data “de-identified” in front of a privacy officer will cost you credibility. Call it redacted or masked and treat it as still-sensitive. That vocabulary distinction is small and it signals a lot.

The state-law layer exists. HIPAA is a floor, not a ceiling; state privacy laws can be stricter, and a national virtual-care operation touches many of them. You do not need to know the details. You need to not claim HIPAA compliance is the whole answer.

Takeaways
  • The exposure is concentrated in logs and tickets, not source code — which is exactly where two of the three role goals point
  • BAA coverage is per-feature, not per-vendor. Ask which feature, in which configuration
  • Minimum necessary is why scoped access beats ambient access architecturally, not just prudentially
  • Say redacted or masked, not de-identified, unless Safe Harbor or Expert Determination was actually done

Who signs a BAA — and what it actually covers

The module that makes you sound like you have done this before
By the end of this module you will
  • Know, per vendor, what is covered and what is explicitly excluded — from primary sources
  • Be able to explain the zero-data-retention paradox, which almost nobody outside this problem knows about
  • Know which commonly-repeated vendor claims are wrong, and how to check one yourself in 90 seconds
Read this before you quote any of it

Vendor compliance posture changes frequently, and this page is a snapshot taken 31 August 2026. Everything below is sourced, and I have marked exactly how well. But in an interview the correct register is “as of when I checked, here is the shape of it, and here is how I would verify before committing” — not “X does not sign BAAs.” The shape is durable. The specifics decay.

The zero-data-retention paradox

This is the sharpest thing in this course, and it is genuinely counterintuitive. It concerns Anthropic, but the pattern generalises.

Intuitively, zero data retention (the vendor keeps nothing after serving the request) should be the most compliant configuration. And for Claude Code, it is required:

Verified “Claude Code is covered under your BAA only with zero data retention (ZDR) enabled, and only on qualified accounts. Without ZDR, Claude Code remains available to use but isn't covered.” Anthropic Help Center, HIPAA-ready Enterprise plans

But Anthropic also designates certain newer frontier models as Covered Models, which carry a safety-driven retention requirement that runs the other way:

Verified Covered Models “require 30-day data retention on every platform where they're offered, as part of our safety work” and “can't be accessed from organizations or workspaces with zero data retention (ZDR) enabled.” Anthropic Help Center, Covered Models under a BAA
The collision

Verified Anthropic states it directly: “There's currently no configuration that allows BAA-covered access to Covered Models in Claude Code or Cowork.”

Read that twice. Claude Code needs ZDR to be BAA-covered. Covered Models refuse to run under ZDR. Therefore the newest models and BAA-covered Claude Code are mutually exclusive. Per Anthropic, only Chat on HIPAA-ready Enterprise plans and the HIPAA-ready API support BAA coverage and Covered Model access simultaneously. In a ZDR org, requests to a Covered Model return an error.

Opinion Why this is such a good thing to know: it is a real, current, non-obvious constraint that you can only know by having read the documentation rather than the marketing. It also demonstrates the general principle better than any abstraction — safety retention requirements and privacy retention requirements can point in opposite directions, and a healthcare org can get caught in the gap. If you say this in the room, say it as a shape, not a gotcha: “the newest models often carry retention obligations for safety monitoring, which can conflict with the zero-retention posture you want for PHI. So ‘we'll just use the best model' is not always available to us.”

A worked example of why you check primary sources

While researching this module I found a confident secondary claim that AWS's HIPAA Eligible Services reference lists Amazon Bedrock with an explicit exclusion for certain frontier model families.

I fetched the actual AWS page. It does not. Bedrock and Bedrock AgentCore are listed with no model-specific exclusions noted. The only carve-out of that kind visible on the page is Kiro's exclusion of Kiro Web.

The secondary source was wrong, and it was wrong in the direction that made the writer's point more dramatic. Goal 1 — engineers authoring code with AI

The landscape, and the genuinely awkward evidence on whether it works
By the end of this module you will
  • Know the current tool categories and what distinguishes them architecturally
  • Be able to cite the strongest evidence against AI coding productivity, accurately and fairly
  • Know why lines-of-code and acceptance-rate metrics mislead, and what to measure instead

The landscape, by architecture rather than brand

Tools sort into three shapes. Knowing the shapes matters more than knowing the logos, because the shape determines the security surface.

ShapeWhat it isExamplesSecurity surface
Completion / assistantPlugin in your existing editor. Suggests as you type, answers in a side panel.GitHub CopilotNarrow: the open file plus some context. Easiest to reason about.
AI-native IDEThe editor rebuilt around the model. Whole-repo indexing, multi-file edits.CursorWider: it indexes the repo. Whatever PHI is in fixtures is now in an index.
Terminal / agenticRuns as an agent with tool access — reads files, runs commands, edits, iterates.Claude Code, CodexWidest: it can run commands. If it can run kubectl logs, it can reach production PHI.

Reported On adoption: JetBrains' Developer Ecosystem Survey, fielded May–July 2026 across more than 15,000 professional developers, reports 90% using AI coding agents at work at least weekly and 68% daily, with Claude Code described as the most widely adopted AI coding tool at work.

Opinion The operational consequence of that number is the one worth saying: at 90% weekly usage industry-wide, you are not deciding whether to adopt AI coding tools. You are deciding whether the ones already in use are the sanctioned ones. In a HIPAA org, unsanctioned adoption is not a productivity question, it is an unreported disclosure. The first deliverable of an enablement role is usually not a rollout — it is an amnesty and an inventory.

The evidence, told honestly

This is where most candidates either over-claim or have not read anything. Do neither.

The METR randomised controlled trial — state it precisely

Verified METR ran an RCT with 16 experienced open-source developers across 246 real tasks in repositories they already maintained. Issues were randomly assigned to allow or disallow AI tooling.

Result: developers took 19% longer when allowed to use AI tools.

The perception gap is the part that matters most. Beforehand, developers forecast AI would make them 24% faster. Afterwards — having actually been slowed down — they still believed AI had made them 20% faster.

Now state the caveats, because they are the authors' own

Being fair to this study is as important as citing it, and the authors are unusually careful. They explicitly state their work does not show that AI fails to speed up most developers. Their stated limitations include:

  • 16 developers, all working in large, mature repositories they knew deeply (high context, high quality bar) — close to the hardest case for an assistant
  • Findings are not claimed to generalise beyond software development
  • The tooling was early-2025 frontier — primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. Tools have moved substantially since
  • They do not rule out that better prompting, scaffolding or training could produce speedup
  • Possible sampling bias: developers who felt AI already helped them may have declined to participate

Opinion Anyone who cites the 19% as proof that AI coding tools do not work is misusing it. The robust reading is narrower and more useful: expert developers in codebases they know well are poor judges of their own speedup, and the direction of the error is consistently optimistic. That claim survives all the caveats, and it is the one that should change how you measure.

What DORA found, and where its numbers come from

Verified DORA's central framing in its 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development report: “AI's primary role is as an amplifier, magnifying an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses,” with the greatest returns coming “not from the tools themselves, but from a strategic focus on the underlying organizational system.”

Verified DORA published a follow-up, ROI of AI-assisted Software Development (updated 22 April 2026), which addresses what its own page calls “the initial ‘productivity dip’ of a new rollout.”

Reported Via InfoQ's coverage of that report, three concepts worth having in your vocabulary:

The J-curve
Value from an AI rollout gets worse before it gets better. The dip is attributed to learning curves, the verification tax, and downstream processes not yet adapted to higher code volume. The report reportedly calls this “the tuition cost of transformation.”
Why you want this: it lets you pre-commit leadership to a dip instead of being surprised by one. A rollout that dips and was predicted to dip is on track. The same rollout unpredicted is a failure.
The verification tax
The additional effort of checking whether AI-generated code is correct, secure and architecturally coherent. It does not go away with better models; it moves from writing to reviewing.
Why you want this: it explains the METR result mechanically. Generation got cheap; verification did not.
Tokenmaxxing
Tracking and rewarding raw AI token consumption — internal leaderboards for who used the most AI. Reported as an emerging trend and a trap: it can nudge hesitant developers to try the tools, but treating token spend as a performance indicator is optimising the input and calling it the output.
Why you want this: someone will propose exactly this. Having a name for it and a reason it fails is worth a great deal in that meeting.
Be careful with DORA's ROI numbers — they are modelled, not measured

Modelled Coverage of the ROI report cites figures like roughly 39% first-year ROI for a 500-person organisation, about $11.6M return against $8.4M investment, and an eight-month payback. These are outputs of the report's sample ROI calculator under stated assumptions — a worked example, not an observed outcome across real companies.

Unconfirmed A figure of “727% ROI over three years” also circulates attached to this topic. That is a vendor-commissioned Google Cloud AI figure, not a DORA research finding, and it should not be presented as one.

If you quote a number in the interview, quote METR's 19% — it is measured, primary, and it makes you sound sceptical rather than sold. Use DORA for the concepts (amplifier, J-curve, verification tax), which is where its value is anyway.

Why the obvious metrics mislead

MetricWhy it is temptingWhy it misleads
Lines of code generatedEasy, big, goes upMeasures volume, not value. AI reliably increases volume. Volume is a cost.
Suggestion acceptance rateThe vendor dashboard shows itMeasures whether the tool guessed the next token, not whether the change shipped or was correct. Optimisable by accepting more junk.
Self-reported time savedCheap to collectMETR is a direct measurement of this being wrong — and wrong optimistically, by roughly 39 percentage points between belief and reality.
Seats provisioned / licences activeProcurement wants itMeasures spend, not outcome. The tokenmaxxing failure in a different costume.
PRs merged per engineerFeels like throughputCloser, but degrades under Goodhart and ignores the instability side of DORA's finding.

What to measure instead

DORA's four keys — deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore — give you throughput and stability. That pairing is the point: DORA's 2025 finding is that AI adoption can raise throughput while raising instability, so measuring only the first half will make a rollout look like a success while it is degrading reliability.

SPACE Verified (Forsgren, Storey and colleagues, 2021) adds the dimensions delivery metrics cannot see: Satisfaction and well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication and collaboration, Efficiency and flow. Its central claim is that productivity is not capturable in a single metric and must be read across dimensions simultaneously.

The answer to “how would you measure success?”

“Four keys for the delivery system, SPACE for the human system, and one honest qualitative signal — would you be annoyed if we took this away? I would deliberately not report acceptance rate or lines generated, because both go up when the tool is doing badly. And I would pre-commit to a J-curve so that the first eight weeks looking worse is a prediction rather than a crisis.”

That answer does four things at once: it names the standard frameworks, it rejects the vendor metrics, it shows you know throughput and stability trade off, and it inoculates against the dip.

Takeaways
  • Three shapes — completion, AI-native IDE, terminal agent — with widening security surfaces. The shape determines the PHI question
  • METR: 16 devs, 246 tasks, 19% slower, forecast 24% faster, still believed 20% faster afterwards. Cite it with the authors' caveats
  • DORA: AI is an amplifier; expect a J-curve and a verification tax. Use DORA's concepts, not its modelled ROI figures
  • Measure four keys + SPACE. Never acceptance rate, never lines, never self-reported time saved
  • At ~90% industry usage, your first job is an inventory and an amnesty, not a rollout

Goal 2 — operational debugging agents

The sharpest version of the problem: logs are where the PHI lives
By the end of this module you will
  • Be able to describe what an incident-triage agent actually does, step by step
  • Know exactly what access it needs and why each grant is dangerous here
  • Have a concrete safety envelope you can propose in the interview
Why this is the hard one

Of the three goals in the role brief, this is the one where the compliance constraint is most severe and least appreciated. An operational debugging agent is, by definition, a system that reads production logs and telemetry. In a healthcare company, production logs are among the most PHI-dense artefacts in the building — and unlike a database, nobody curates them, nobody schema-controls them, and their contents change every time an engineer adds a debug line.

Opinion If you say only one thing about this use case, say this: “Logs are the last place I would point an unvetted model, and the first place everyone wants to.”

What the agent actually does

Stripped of marketing, an incident-triage agent is a loop over the work a human on-call engineer does in the first fifteen minutes:

#StepWhat it needsPHI risk
1Detect — ingest the alert, page, or error-rate spikeRead: alerting systemLow
2Gather — pull recent logs, traces, metrics for the affected service and windowRead: logging, tracing, metricsSevere
3Correlate — what deployed recently? what else is alerting? is this a known issue?Read: deploy history, incident history, ticketsModerate
4Hypothesise — propose a likely cause with the evidence for itModel inferenceInherits step 2
5Summarise — post a structured triage note to the incident channelWrite: chat / incident toolSevere — exfiltration path
6Remediate — roll back, scale, restart, toggle a flagWrite: production control planeDifferent risk class entirely
The step everyone under-thinks: step 5

Steps 2 and 6 get all the attention — reading production data and touching production. But step 5 is the quiet exfiltration path. An agent that reads PHI-bearing logs and then writes a summary into a Slack channel has just moved PHI from a controlled, access-audited, retention-managed store into a chat tool with different retention, broader membership, and probably a different BAA posture.

And it will do it helpfully. “The failing requests all share member ID 4417 and date of birth 1984-03-02” is a genuinely useful triage note and a reportable disclosure.

Opinion This observation is the single best thing you can bring to a discussion of this use case, because it shows you are reasoning about the whole data path rather than the model call.

Where these agents break, independent of compliance

Worth knowing so you are not selling a fantasy:

  • Confident wrong causes. The agent finds a correlation and narrates it as the cause. During an incident, a plausible wrong hypothesis is worse than no hypothesis, because humans anchor on it under time pressure.
  • Novel incidents are the ones that matter. Agents do well on incidents resembling past incidents — which are the ones a runbook already covers. The genuinely novel outage is where you need help most and where retrieval helps least.
  • Context window versus log volume. A busy service produces more log volume in the relevant window than fits in any context. So something must select — and the selector is now the most important and least examined component in the system.
  • Alert fatigue, transposed. An agent that comments on every alert becomes noise that people learn to scroll past, taking real signal with it.
  • Cascading automation. An agent with remediation rights that misdiagnoses can turn a degradation into an outage. Rollback is not always safe; a rollback across a migration boundary can be worse than the bug.

The safety envelope — the concrete proposal

This is what to sketch on the whiteboard if they ask you to design it. Four layers, in order.

LayerControlWhy
1. Redact at the boundary PHI detection and tokenisation before the data leaves your trust boundary — not in a prompt instruction. Replace identifiers with stable tokens (MEMBER_A17) so the agent can still correlate “same member across these five log lines” without ever seeing who they are. Preserves the analytical signal the agent needs while removing the identifiers it does not. Stable tokenisation is the key trick — naive redaction to [REDACTED] destroys the correlation that makes triage work.
2. Scope the grant One service, one time window, read-only, per incident. Not standing access to the logging platform. This is minimum necessary implemented as architecture rather than policy.
3. Constrain the output path The agent writes to the incident record, not to a general channel. Output passes the same redaction filter on the way out as the input did on the way in. Closes the step-5 exfiltration path above.
4. Read-only first, for a long time No remediation rights in v1. Earn write access per action type, with an approval step, after you have measured hypothesis accuracy against what the incident actually turned out to be. You cannot justify autonomous production action until you have data on how often it is right. Collect that data in shadow mode.
The strongest version of the proposal — and it borrows their own language

“I would run it in shadow mode against closed incidents first. It produces a hypothesis, nobody sees it during the incident, and afterwards we score it against the actual root cause. That gives us a real accuracy number before anyone trusts it, and it gives us the redaction pipeline exercised on production data with zero blast radius.

It is the same discipline as the clinician-in-the-loop review in your Blueprint paper — 100% human review while the system earns its confidence interval. I would want an engineer-in-the-loop equivalent before it posts anything into a live incident.”

Opinion Shadow mode is the answer to almost every “how would you de-risk this?” question, and it is genuinely the right answer here: it produces the evidence and the pipeline hardening simultaneously, at zero risk.

The better first project, honestly

Opinion If asked what you would actually do first, consider saying this: the highest-value early work here may not be the agent at all. It may be fixing the logs.

A PHI-redaction layer at the logging boundary is valuable on its own compliance merits, is probably already on someone's roadmap, needs no model, and is the hard prerequisite for every subsequent AI use case that touches operational data. Ship that, and the debugging agent becomes a comparatively easy follow-on. Lead with the agent, and you will spend six months in privacy review with nothing shipped.

That answer signals sequencing judgement, which is the actual competence being hired for.

Takeaways
  • Six steps; steps 2 and 5 are the PHI-severe ones. Step 5 — the summary write-out — is the underrated exfiltration path
  • Failure modes: confident wrong causes, weakness on novel incidents, the unexamined log selector, alert fatigue, cascading automation
  • Envelope: tokenise at the boundary (stable tokens, not [REDACTED]) → scope per incident → constrain output path → read-only for a long time
  • Shadow mode against closed incidents is the de-risking answer, and it echoes their own clinician-review methodology
  • The best first project may be the redaction layer, not the agent

Goal 3 — AI for Jira triage

The boring one. Start here anyway.
By the end of this module you will
  • Know the four distinct jobs hiding inside “triage” and which is worth doing first
  • Be able to say concretely what good looks like, with numbers
  • Know the PHI catch that makes this less trivial than it sounds

Why the boring one goes first

Opinion Ticket triage is unglamorous, and it is the strongest opening move of the three. The reasons compound:

  • The ground truth already exists. Every historical ticket has a final component, priority and assignee. You have a labelled dataset for free and can measure accuracy before shipping anything — which is not true of the other two goals.
  • The failure mode is cheap. A misrouted ticket costs one hop. A wrong incident hypothesis costs an outage.
  • The value is legible to non-engineers. “Time to first correct assignee” is a number a VP understands without a framework.
  • It builds the compliance scaffolding you need later. Redaction, BAA-covered inference, audit logging — all get exercised on a low-stakes workload, so the hard use cases inherit a proven pipeline.

The four jobs inside “triage”

JobWhat it doesMeasurable asDifficulty
ClassificationComponent, type, severity, affected product area Accuracy vs. the final human-assigned valueEasiest
RoutingWhich team or individualReassignment rate; time to first correct ownerEasy
DeduplicationIs this the same as an existing ticket? Duplicate pairs caught; false-merge rateModerate — embeddings plus a threshold
SummarisationCompress a 40-comment thread into a state-of-play Hard to score automatically; use human preferenceModerate, and the least valuable of the four

Opinion Start with classification and routing. They are the two with clean ground truth, and together they produce the metric everyone cares about: how long until the ticket reaches someone who can act on it. Deduplication is the sleeper — often the largest real time saving, because duplicate incident tickets during a live issue are pure waste — but the false-merge failure is annoying enough that it should follow, not lead. Summarisation demos best and delivers least.

What good actually looks like

>85%routing accuracy vs. final human assignee, before you ship
<2median reassignment hops, down from whatever the baseline is
<1%false-merge rate on dedup — the metric that kills trust
100%of suggestions reversible in one click, with the reason shown
The design principle that decides whether people keep it

Suggest, do not assign — and always show the reason. A ticket that arrives pre-tagged with “Billing / P2 — matched on 'claim rejected' + 'EOB', similar to PROJ-4471” and a one-click override is a gift. A ticket that was silently reassigned to the wrong team and sat there for two days is why the team turns the feature off in week three.

Opinion This is the same principle as the debugging agent's read-only stance and the same principle as clinician-in-the-loop. Notice the pattern — every safe AI deployment in this company is going to be shaped like “the system proposes, a human disposes, and the proposal is auditable.” If you can articulate that as a general design stance, you have the thesis of the whole role in one sentence.

The PHI catch

Support-originated tickets are not clean

PHI An internal engineering ticket is usually low-risk. A ticket escalated from member support is frequently full of PHI: the member's name, DOB, the clinical reason they called, a screenshot of their record, a pasted chat transcript.

So triage inherits the same boundary problem as the debugging agent, with a wrinkle: the classification signal and the PHI are often the same words. “Member could not book an oncology follow-up” is both the routing signal and a clinical disclosure.

Practical answer: redact identifiers (name, DOB, MRN, contact details) and preserve clinical category terms, since the category is what routes the ticket and is far less identifying on its own. Then confirm that judgement with the privacy team rather than assuming it — “I would want to check that with privacy” is a strong thing to say, not a weak one.

A cheap architectural point worth making

Classification and routing do not need a frontier model. A small model, or even embeddings plus a classifier trained on your own historical tickets, will often beat a large general model on your taxonomy — because your taxonomy is idiosyncratic and your history encodes it.

Two consequences that matter here: it is dramatically cheaper per ticket, and a smaller model is easier to run inside your own trust boundary, which shrinks the compliance surface to nearly nothing. Opinion “This one probably should not use an external frontier model at all” is a sophisticated thing to be able to say in an AI enablement interview, precisely because it is not the expected answer.

Takeaways
  • Four jobs: classification, routing, dedup, summarisation. Start with classification + routing — clean ground truth, legible metric
  • Ground truth is free: every historical ticket has its final component, priority and assignee
  • Suggest, do not assign. Always show the reason. One-click override.
  • Support-escalated tickets are PHI-dense, and the routing signal and the PHI are often the same words
  • Frequently the right answer is a small in-house model, not a frontier API — cheaper and almost no compliance surface

Speaking the language

The distinctions that matter, and the ones that are marketing
By the end of this module you will
  • Be able to use the current vocabulary precisely, including where terms are genuinely contested
  • Know which distinctions are real engineering and which are vendor positioning
  • Have a defensible answer on fine-tuning vs. retrieval vs. prompting
The meta-skill

Fluency is not knowing more words. It is knowing which words are load-bearing and which are contested. Someone who says “agent” as though it has one settled meaning sounds less expert than someone who says “agent, in the sense of a model with tool access and a control loop — people also use it for a scripted workflow with one model call, so it is worth pinning down which we mean.”

Terms below marked contested have no settled definition. Saying so is a signal of expertise, not uncertainty.

The core vocabulary

Agent
Working definition: a model given tools, run in a loop, with some autonomy over which action comes next. The loop and the autonomy are what distinguish it from a single model call. Why it is contested: vendors apply it to everything from a scripted two-step chain to a system that runs unsupervised for hours. The useful discriminator is not the label but two questions: what tools can it call, and who decides when it stops?
Say: “When I say agent I mean tool access plus a control loop. The security question is entirely about the tool surface — the model is the least interesting part.”
Tool use / function calling
The model emits a structured request to call a named function with typed arguments; your code executes it and returns the result. The model never executes anything itself — it asks, your harness decides. That distinction is the entire basis of agent security: every real control lives in the harness, not in the prompt.
Say: “The model requests; the harness decides. So permissioning belongs in the tool layer, not in prompt instructions — prompts are not a security boundary.”
MCP (Model Context Protocol)
An open protocol standardising how models connect to tools and data sources — roughly, a common interface so every tool integration is not bespoke per vendor. Practically: a growing ecosystem of servers exposing systems to models. The part that matters here: an MCP server is a data egress path. Anthropic's BAA documentation states that third-party integration data flows, MCP included, are not covered by their BAA. So MCP is an architecture question and a compliance question simultaneously.
Say: “MCP standardises the connection, it does not standardise the trust. Every server is a new egress path that needs its own review.”
RAG vs. long context
RAG retrieves relevant chunks and puts them in the prompt. Long context puts the whole corpus in and lets attention find it. Frontier context windows now reach ~1M tokens, which has led to “RAG is dead” claims. Why that is wrong, and the reason is not what people usually say: the good arguments for retrieval in a healthcare org are cost (you pay per token, every call), latency, and above all access control — retrieval is where you enforce that this user may see these documents. Stuffing everything into context means every query carries the whole corpus past the permission boundary. In a PHI environment, retrieval is a security control that happens to also help accuracy.
Say: “Long context reduced how much retrieval we need for quality. It did not remove the need for retrieval as an access-control point — which is the reason we would keep it here regardless.”
Context engineering
The discipline of deciding what goes into the model's context window and in what form: system instructions, retrieved documents, tool definitions, conversation history, and what to evict when it fills. Why it is contested: it is partly a genuine rebranding of a real discipline that outgrew “prompt engineering,” and partly a way to make prompt-writing sound like architecture. Opinion The substance is real — context is a scarce, expensive, attention-limited resource and managing it is most of the work in a production agent. The term is also doing marketing work. Both can be true.
Say: “Context is a budget. Most agent quality problems I have seen are context problems, not model problems.”
Evals
Systematic measurement of output quality against a fixed dataset — the test suite of AI systems. Because outputs are non-deterministic, you measure distributions, not assertions. Types: exact-match on labelled data (cheap, needs ground truth), LLM-as-judge (scalable, needs its own validation against human judgement), and human review (expensive, the ground truth everything else is calibrated against). The healthcare-specific point: your eval set is itself a PHI artefact if built from production data. This is the strongest use case for synthetic data — generate realistic non-real tickets, logs and cases so the eval set can live outside the PHI boundary and be shared freely with vendors.
Say: “No evals, no deployment. And in our case the eval set should be synthetic so it is not itself a compliance artefact.”
Inference cost
You pay per token, split into input and output, with output typically several times the input price. Levers, roughly in order of free-ness: prompt caching (reuse a stable prefix across calls — often the largest single saving and costs you nothing in quality), trimming input, capping output, batch processing for non-urgent work, then the quality trade-offs: reasoning effort settings, and finally model choice. The counterintuitive bit: judge cost per completed task, not per request. A cheaper model that needs three attempts and a human fix is not cheaper.
Say: “Caching first, model choice last. And measure cost per completed task, not per call — otherwise you optimise into a cheaper model that costs more.”

Fine-tuning vs. retrieval vs. prompting

A standard interview question. The wrong answer treats them as competing options on one axis.

ApproachChangesUse whenCost / risk here
PromptingInstructions onlyAlways first. Behaviour, format, tone, task framing Near zero. Iterate in minutes.
Retrieval (RAG)What the model knows right now The model needs facts it was not trained on: your runbooks, your ticket history, your schemas. Facts change. Moderate. And it is where access control lives.
Fine-tuningThe model's learned behaviour Consistent format or style at scale; a narrow classification task where you have a lot of labels; latency or cost pressure that a smaller tuned model solves High here. Training on PHI creates a model artefact derived from PHI — a governance object with its own storage, access, retention and deletion questions, and a memorisation risk. Ask whether the vendor will even train under a BAA.
The answer to give

“They solve different problems. Prompting changes behaviour, retrieval changes available knowledge, fine-tuning changes learned behaviour. The order is prompt, then retrieve, then fine-tune — and you should have an eval in place before you do the third, or you cannot tell whether it helped.

In a HIPAA context there is an extra reason to defer fine-tuning: a model fine-tuned on PHI is itself a PHI-derived artefact. That is a governance object with retention and deletion obligations, and you need to answer 'what happens when a member exercises a deletion right' before you create one, not after.”

Terms to use carefully

TermThe problemBetter
“Hallucination”Anthropomorphises and implies a malfunction. The model is doing exactly what it does; it has no truth oracle.“Confabulation”, or plainly: “confidently wrong output”
“The model decided”Obscures that your harness executed it. Matters enormously in an incident review.“The model requested X and our harness executed it”
“De-identified”Has a specific HIPAA meaning — Safe Harbor or Expert Determination. Regex scrubbing is neither.“Redacted” / “masked” / “tokenised”
“HIPAA compliant” (of a tool)Tools are not compliant; deployments are. A tool can be HIPAA-eligible.“HIPAA-eligible, and covered under our BAA for these specific features”
“Autonomous”Almost always means “autonomous within a tool surface someone chose.” Name the actual authority: “can open a PR, cannot merge”
Takeaways
  • Agent = tools + loop + autonomy. The tool surface is the security question, not the model
  • The model requests, the harness decides. Prompts are not a security boundary
  • Retrieval survives long context because it is where access control lives — a security argument, not just a quality one
  • Order: prompt → retrieve → fine-tune. Fine-tuning on PHI creates a PHI-derived governance artefact
  • Evals should be synthetic here, so the eval set is not itself a compliance object
  • Tools are HIPAA-eligible; deployments are compliant

The rollout playbook

Pilots, champions, procurement, and what to say when leadership wants a number you cannot honestly produce
By the end of this module you will
  • Have a defensible 90-day sequence you can describe from memory
  • Know how to run security review as a partnership rather than a gate
  • Have a genuine answer to the productivity-number question — the hardest question in this interview

Pilot or org-wide?

Pilot first when

The compliance surface is unresolved — which here it is.

You need evidence to win an argument you have not won yet.

The failure mode is expensive or hard to reverse.

You do not yet know which teams have the appetite.

Go broad when

Shadow adoption is already widespread — a pilot is theatre if 60% of engineers already use it.

The tool is low-risk and per-seat cost is the only variable.

A narrow pilot would create a have/have-not dynamic that poisons the rollout.

Opinion The nuance worth voicing: for AI coding tools in 2026, the pilot-versus-rollout framing is often already obsolete. At ~90% industry-wide weekly usage, the realistic starting position is that people are using something, possibly personal accounts, possibly with company code in it. In a HIPAA org that is an open compliance question, not a productivity opportunity. So the honest first move is: find out what is already happening, without punishing anyone for telling you. Then sanction the good paths quickly, because the alternative to a sanctioned tool is not no tool.

A 90-day sequence

WindowDoDeliverable
Days 1–15Inventory and amnesty. What is in use, on whose account, touching what data? Explicitly no-blame. In parallel, read their clinical AI governance framework and find out who owns it. An honest map, and a relationship with privacy/security started before you need something from them
Days 15–30Agree the data-sensitivity tiers (Module 3) with security and privacy. Not a tool decision — a framework decision. A tiering doc they co-own. This is the single highest-leverage artefact of the whole plan.

Why this order, if asked

Each step buys permission for the next. The inventory buys credibility with security. The tiering buys a repeatable decision process instead of per-tool arguments. The coding rollout buys visible value. The triage classifier buys a real number. And only then do you ask for access to production logs — with a track record, a co-owned framework, and a redaction pipeline already proven on a low-stakes workload.

Opinion Doing it in the order the role brief lists them — coding, debugging agents, Jira — would put the hardest compliance ask second, before you have any credibility to spend. Saying so, politely, is a good interview moment.

Champions

  • Recruit sceptics, not enthusiasts. Enthusiasts convert nobody — their endorsement is priced in. A respected sceptic who says “I tried it properly for three weeks and it earns its place on this kind of task, not that kind” moves an entire org.
  • One per team, and give them real air cover: time budgeted, a direct line to you, early access, and their name on the outcome.
  • Make them the reporting channel for failures. If your champions only bring you wins, you have advocates, not instrumentation.
  • Beware the demo trap. The engineer who makes the best demo is often not the one whose judgement about production usage you should trust.

Procurement and security review

The instinct is to treat security review as a gate to get through. That is the mistake, and in this company it is an expensive one — because their published position is that AI safety governance is a differentiator they write papers about.

DoInstead of

The framing that makes security an ally

“My job is not to get tools past you. It is to make sure that when engineers use AI — which they already are — they do it on a path we chose together and can audit. The risk I am managing is not that we adopt too slowly. It is that we adopt invisibly.”

This is true, it is the actual risk, and it reframes you from supplicant to co-owner.

The hardest question: “what is the productivity gain?”

Leadership will want a number. Early on, an honest one does not exist. Three ways to answer, in ascending order of quality:

Bad: make one up from vendor material
“Studies show 30–50% productivity gains.” The METR RCT measured 19% slower in a rigorous setting. If anyone in the room knows that, your credibility is gone — and in a company that publishes peer-reviewed methodology papers, assume someone does.
Weak: refuse
“You can't measure developer productivity.” True-ish, and it reads as evasion. It also leaves leadership with no way to decide, so they will decide with someone else's number instead of yours.
Good: reframe to a decision, commit to a date
Give them what a number is for — a decision — plus a specific commitment about when a real one arrives.
The answer, close to verbatim

“I can give you a number in 90 days that I would defend, and I can tell you right now what it will not be.

It will not be a percentage productivity gain, because the best evidence we have says engineers mis-estimate their own AI speedup by a wide margin, and optimistically — there is a randomised trial where developers were 19% slower and still believed they had been 20% faster. So any number built on self-report or on lines generated is worse than no number.

What I will give you is: change failure rate and time to restore before and after, so we know we did not trade throughput for stability; time to first correct assignee on tickets, which is a directly measured saving; and retention of usage — who is still using this in week twelve without being asked. And I would ask for one thing in return: agree now that the first six to eight weeks will look flat or slightly worse. That dip is well documented, it is a real cost of the transition, and if we have not agreed on it in advance it will get read as failure right at the point where we should be holding our nerve.”

Opinion That answer works because it does four things a vendor-number answer cannot: it demonstrates you have read the literature, it refuses to be dishonest, it still gives leadership a decision-useful commitment, and it pre-negotiates the J-curve. Pre-negotiating the dip is the highest-leverage sentence in the whole answer — most AI initiatives are cancelled during the dip, by people who were never told it was coming.

Takeaways
  • Start with inventory and amnesty — shadow adoption is the real starting position
  • The tiering framework co-owned with security is the highest-leverage artefact you produce
  • Sequence buys permission: inventory → tiering → coding rollout → triage number → then ask for logs
  • Recruit sceptics as champions; make them your failure-reporting channel
  • On the number: refuse the fake one, commit to a real one with a date, and pre-negotiate the dip

Tool 2 — interview drill

Answer each out loud before opening the model answer. The model answers are deliberately written the way you would actually say them, not the way you would write them down.

“How would you get our engineers using AI coding tools?”
Model answer

“My first assumption is that they already are — industry surveys this year put weekly usage around 90%, and I would be surprised if you were an exception. So the first two weeks are an inventory and an amnesty: what is actually in use, on whose account, touching what data, with nobody punished for answering honestly.

That matters more here than at a normal software company, because unsanctioned usage in a HIPAA environment is not a productivity gap, it is a potential unreported disclosure. Once I know the real starting position, the job is to make the sanctioned path better than the shadow path — properly configured, BAA-covered, and fast to get access to. The alternative to a sanctioned tool is never no tool.”

“What is the risk of an agent that reads our production logs?”
Model answer

“Logs are probably the most PHI-dense uncontrolled artefact you have — request bodies on error, stack traces carrying member objects, debug lines someone left on. So the read side is the obvious risk.

But the one I would flag is the write side. An agent that reads those logs and then posts a helpful triage summary into an incident channel has just moved PHI out of an access-audited, retention-managed store into a chat tool with different retention and broader membership. And it will do it helpfully — ‘all the failing requests share this member ID and DOB’ is a genuinely useful triage note and a disclosure.

So: tokenise at the boundary with stable tokens, so it can still correlate 'same member' without knowing who; scope access to one service and one window per incident rather than standing access; run the output through the same filter as the input; and keep it read-only far longer than feels necessary. I would run it in shadow mode against closed incidents first so we get a real accuracy number before anyone trusts it during a live one.”

“Which AI vendor should we use?”
Model answer

“I would not answer that as a vendor question first, because the answer changes per use case and it changes every quarter. I would want a data-sensitivity tiering that we agree once with security — green for no-PHI-possible work, amber for repo code that could contain PHI in fixtures, red for anything touching logs or tickets — and then the vendor question becomes a lookup instead of an argument each time.

What I would bring to that conversation is that BAA coverage is per-feature, not per-vendor. As a concrete example: Anthropic covers their Messages API under a BAA but explicitly excludes the Batch API, Files API, Code Execution and Computer Use. And there is a real trap in there — Claude Code is only BAA-covered with zero data retention enabled, but their newest models require 30-day retention for safety monitoring and refuse to run under ZDR. So those two things are currently mutually exclusive. That is the kind of detail that decides an architecture, and you only find it by reading the coverage docs rather than the marketing.

Practically, if you are already on AWS or GCP, the lowest-drama path for anything red is inference through Bedrock or Vertex under the cloud BAA you already have.”

“Leadership wants to know the productivity gain. What do you tell them?”
Model answer

See the near-verbatim version in Module 8 — but the shape is: refuse the fake number, name why self-report is specifically unreliable (the METR trial: 19% slower, and participants still believed they were 20% faster), commit to specific measured metrics on a date, and pre-negotiate the J-curve so the initial dip reads as a prediction rather than a failure.

The pre-negotiation is the part most people miss, and it is the part that actually saves the programme.”

“An engineer wants to paste a production error into ChatGPT to debug it faster. What do you do?”
Model answer

“In the moment: no, if it is a personal account and the payload could contain PHI — that is a disclosure to a vendor we have no BAA with, and it is reportable.

But I would treat that request as a bug report about my own work rather than a policy violation to enforce. That engineer had a real need and the sanctioned path was either too slow or did not exist. If the answer is only 'no', they will do it anyway and stop telling me.

So the real fix is a sanctioned path that is genuinely fast: a BAA-covered endpoint, reachable from where they already are, with redaction at the boundary so the paste is safe by construction rather than by the engineer's judgement at 2am during an incident. Judgement is the wrong control to rely on at 2am.”

“What would you ship first?”
Model answer

“Jira triage — classification and routing — and I would argue for it over the more exciting options.

Every historical ticket already has its final component, priority and assignee, so I have a labelled dataset for free and can measure accuracy offline before anything touches production. The failure mode is one extra routing hop rather than an outage. The metric — time to first correct assignee — is legible to people who do not care about AI. And it exercises the compliance scaffolding I will need later, redaction and audit and BAA-covered inference, on a low-stakes workload.

Then the harder ones inherit a proven pipeline and I have a track record when I ask for access to production logs. Doing it in the other order means making the biggest compliance ask before I have any credibility to spend. There is also a decent chance triage should not use a frontier model at all — embeddings plus a classifier on your own ticket history will often beat a general model on your own taxonomy, cost far less, and run inside your boundary.”

“How do you know it is working?”
Model answer

“DORA's four keys for the delivery system — deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore — because DORA's own finding is that AI adoption can lift throughput while raising instability, so measuring only speed will make a rollout look successful while reliability degrades. SPACE for the human dimensions delivery metrics cannot see.

And deliberately not: suggestion acceptance rate, lines generated, or self-reported time saved. All three go up when the tool is doing badly, and the third is the one the METR trial directly measured as wrong.

Plus one qualitative signal I trust more than it deserves: at week twelve, who is still using it without being asked, and who would be annoyed if we took it away.”

“What is your biggest worry about all this?”
Model answer

“That we optimise the visible half. Generation got cheap and verification did not — DORA calls that the verification tax — so the risk is that we ship more code faster, feel successful, and quietly push the cost into review, testing and incident load where nobody attributes it back to the AI programme.

The compliance version of the same worry is worse: the failure would not be a dramatic breach, it would be a slow accumulation of PHI in places nobody catalogued — a vendor's logs, a chat channel, an eval set someone built from production data. That is why I would rather spend the first month on an inventory and a tiering framework than on a tool rollout. It is the less impressive answer and I think it is the right one.”

One thing to remember if you remember nothing else

Every safe AI deployment in this company has the same shape: the system proposes, a human disposes, and the proposal is auditable.

Clinician-in-the-loop for their patient-facing assistant. Engineer-in-the-loop for a debugging agent. Suggest-don't-assign for ticket triage. It is one idea wearing three hats, it matches the methodology they published themselves, and if you can name it as a general design stance you have articulated the thesis of the entire role.
