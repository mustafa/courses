Before You Start: The Rules of Engagement

▼

There are two goals here, and only one of them is "talk like your kids." The first goal — the important one — is comprehension. When Rehaan says something at dinner and Zara laughs and you smile like a hostage, that's a gap worth closing. The second goal is limited, surgical production. Maybe six words. You will be tempted to use more. Do not.

The Cringe Risk Scale

Every term in this course carries a rating. It is not about whether the word is bad — it's about whether you, a 40-something VP of Engineering, can say it in front of your children and survive.

🟢 Safe for dadsSay it. It's basically standard English now. Nobody will flinch.
🟡 Once a monthDeployable, but rate-limited. Overuse converts you instantly into a meme.
🔴 Do not attemptYou will understand it. You will never say it. This is fine.
⚫ Know it, never say itParental-awareness vocabulary. Learn it for safety, not for banter.
🎓 The One Rule That Matters

Never explain the slang to your kids. The single fastest way to kill a word is for a parent to define it correctly, at volume, in front of their friends. If you say "bet" and it lands, say nothing. Do not follow up with "which means agreed, right?" You have to let it live in the wild without a footnote. Comprehension is a stealth skill. The moment it becomes visible, it's over.

How the term cards work

Each entry gives you four things:

  • Means — the actual definition, no hedging.
  • In the wild — a sentence you might plausibly hear in your own house.
  • Dad translation — the millennial-era phrase that occupied the same slot in your brain. This is the bit that makes it stick.
  • Note — origin, drift, or the specific way you're about to misuse it.
🧠 A word on where this language comes from

A large share of what gets labelled "Gen Z slang" is African American Vernacular English that's decades old, plus ballroom and drag culture vocabulary from the 1980s–90s, both routed through TikTok and flattened into "kid words." Slay, ate, no cap, bussin, periodt, serving — none of these were invented in 2023. Knowing that isn't a lecture; it's just accurate, and it makes you the rare adult who knows the etymology instead of just the punchline. It's also the difference between using a word and cosplaying it.

Study plan

  1. Read a module. Don't memorize — just get the shape.
  2. Take the quizzes. They're the actual learning mechanism; the reading is scaffolding.
  3. Drill the flashcard deck at the end of each module until you can do it at a red light.
  4. Deploy exactly one term per week. Observe. Adjust.
  5. Come back in six months, because roughly a third of this will be dead.
✅ Your success metric

Not "my kids think I'm cool." That outcome is unreachable and pursuing it is exactly what makes you a chopped unc (Module 7). Your metric is: you understood the joke in real time and didn't ask. That's it. That's the whole win.

01

The Basics — Terms You'll Hear Every Day

▼

These nine words make up an enormous share of everything you're currently not understanding. Learn only this module and your comprehension jumps more than the other seven combined. Several of these have already leaked into adult speech — you have probably heard a colleague say "lowkey" in a planning meeting and thought nothing of it.

No cap

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansNo lie. I'm being completely serious. The opposite, cap, means a lie — and capping means lying.

In the wild"That test was harder than the practice one, no cap."

Dad translation"I swear to God" / "for real though."

Note"Cap" for lying is decades-old AAVE, well documented long before TikTok. The 🧢 emoji is used the same way — sending just a cap emoji is calling someone a liar without typing a word.

Bet

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansOkay. Sure. Deal. Agreed. It's an affirmative — the entire response, one syllable. Depending on tone it can also mean "oh really? watch this."

In the wildYou: "Shoes on, we leave in five." Rehaan: "Bet."

Dad translation"Word." / "Cool." / "You got it."

NoteThis is the single highest-return word for a parent. It's short, it's neutral, and it doesn't require you to perform anything. It is the "sudo" of dad slang.

Fr / fr fr

"for real" / "for real for real"🟡 Once a month

MeansFor real. The doubled version is emphasis — genuinely, no exaggeration. Frequently paired: "no cap fr fr."

In the wild"Zara fr fr practiced for two hours today."

Dad translation"Honestly." / "No, seriously."

NoteTyped constantly, said out loud less often. Written "fr" is safe; saying "fr fr" aloud is where the yellow rating comes from.

Slay

🟡 Once a month

MeansDid something exceptionally well. Also a standalone compliment — you can just say "slay" at someone who looks good or did well.

In the wild"You got 100 on the spelling test? Slay."

Dad translation"Killed it." / "Nailed it."

NoteFrom Black and Latino ballroom culture, 1980s. Went mainstream, got absorbed by corporate LinkedIn posts, and is now used semi-ironically by kids because adults ruined it. Use with awareness that you are late.

Lowkey / Highkey

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansLowkey = slightly, secretly, kind of. Highkey = openly, very much, no shame about it. They're intensity dials on whatever comes next.

In the wild"I lowkey want pizza again." / "I highkey need a nap."

Dad translation"Kind of" / "Not gonna lie, I really…"

NoteAlready fully assimilated into adult professional speech. You can say "lowkey" in a staff meeting and nobody will blink. This is what full linguistic naturalization looks like.

Bussin

🟡 Once a month

MeansReally good. Almost always about food. "Bussin bussin" is the intensified form.

In the wild"Dad's biryani is bussin."

Dad translation"That's fire." / "That slaps." (both of which are also now dad phrases, which is the whole problem)

NoteAAVE origin, went viral via TikTok food videos. Now heavily used ironically. Applying it to non-food ("this spreadsheet is bussin") is a recognized dad move — deploy knowingly or not at all.

It's giving ___

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansIt evokes a specific vibe. The blank takes anything — a noun, an adjective, a whole scenario. "It's giving" with nothing after it means the vibe is strong and self-evident.

In the wild"That jacket is giving substitute teacher." (This will be said about you.)

Dad translation"It's very…" / "That's giving off major ___ energy."

NoteAlso from ballroom culture. Structurally it's one of the most flexible constructions in the language right now — it lets you compare something to a whole aesthetic in three words. Genuinely useful.

Vibe check

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansAn assessment of someone's mood or energy — either a real question ("how are you actually doing") or a joke about being caught in a bad mood. "Failed the vibe check" = gave off bad energy.

In the wild"Vibe check — why is everyone quiet in this car?"

Dad translation"How's everyone doing?" / "Read the room."

NoteOld by internet standards (2019-ish) but stable, and unusually useful for a parent — it's a low-pressure way to ask a teenager how they are without triggering the interrogation reflex.

Periodt

"PURR-ee-udt"🔴 Do not attempt

MeansEnd of discussion. Full stop. Adds finality and attitude to whatever you just said. Sometimes "period, pooh."

In the wild"I'm not doing the dishes tonight. Periodt."

Dad translation"End of story." / "And that's that."

NoteRequires a delivery you do not have. The 't' is doing enormous work and it will not survive contact with a middle-aged voice. Comprehension only.

If you only ever say "bet" and "lowkey", you're already operating above the median dad. Everything else in this module is for listening.

02

The Rizz Dictionary

▼

This module is about charisma as a measurable stat. That framing — social skill as a number you can level up or lose points from — is genuinely new, and it's the thing most worth understanding here. Your kids talk about charm the way you talk about a service-level objective. Almost none of these words are for you to say. Read this one as an anthropologist.

Rizz

🟡 Once a month

MeansCharisma, specifically romantic charm — the ability to attract someone through how you talk to them. Verb form: "to rizz someone up."

In the wild"He walked over and said one sentence and she laughed. Insane rizz."

Dad translation"Game." / "Smooth." / "He's got charm."

NoteClipped from "charisma" — the middle syllable, which is linguistically unusual and part of why it caught on. Popularized by streamer Kai Cenat and named Oxford's Word of the Year in 2023. It has since drifted well past romance: kids now use it for any kind of persuasive charm, including talking a teacher into an extension.

Rizzler

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansA person who has rizz. Often used with mock grandeur, as in "the Rizzler" — a title, not a description.

In the wild"Look at this guy. Absolute Rizzler."

Dad translation"Smooth operator." / "Ladies' man."

NoteNine times out of ten this is sarcasm. Calling yourself the Rizzler is the fastest way to prove you have no rizz — self-declaration voids the claim. That rule holds for basically everything in this module.

W rizz / L rizz

"dub rizz" / "L rizz"🟡 Once a month

MeansW = win, L = loss. So W rizz is charm that worked; L rizz is an attempt that visibly failed. The W/L prefix generalizes to everything: "W dad," "L take," "took the L."

In the wild"He tried a pickup line from a movie. Catastrophic L rizz."

Dad translation"That worked" / "That was a swing and a miss."

NoteThe W/L prefix is the most portable piece of grammar in this entire course. "W dinner" is a real compliment your kid might pay you, and "W" alone in a text is high praise. Learn this one properly.

Unspoken rizz

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansCharisma that requires no words — presence, posture, being the person who doesn't have to try. The highest tier, because effort is the thing that discounts charm.

In the wild"He didn't even talk to her. She came over. Unspoken rizz."

Dad translation"He didn't have to say a word." / "Effortless."

NoteThe value system underneath this is worth clocking: visible effort is penalized. Trying hard and succeeding scores lower than succeeding without trying. This shows up everywhere in Gen Alpha social logic, including at school.

Gyatt

"GYAT" — rhymes with "hot"⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansAn exclamation reacting to someone's body — specifically a large backside. Originally a compressed "goddamn," now used as both an interjection and a noun.

In the wildShouted by an 11-year-old at a screen, with roughly 5% comprehension of what it means.

Dad translationThere isn't one you'd want to say out loud in front of your kids. That's the point.

NoteGenuinely important for a parent to recognize, because a lot of younger kids repeat it without knowing it's about a body. If Zara says it, she's most likely echoing a YouTube edit. Worth a calm conversation, not a scene. Do not ever say this word.

Sigma

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansIndependent, self-directed, doesn't need the group's approval. In practice, a compliment for doing something coolly and alone. "Sigma grindset" = relentless self-improvement, usually mocked.

In the wild"He ate lunch by himself with sunglasses on. Sigma."

Dad translation"Lone wolf." / "Marches to his own beat."

NoteThe important thing for you to know: this arrived from online "manosphere" hierarchy content, and Gen Alpha has largely stripped it down to a joke — kids call a cat sigma. But the word still has a straight-faced version circulating in less friendly corners of the internet. Worth knowing which one is in the room.

Alpha / Beta

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansA supposed social hierarchy: alpha = dominant leader, beta = submissive follower. Used both as a genuine framework in some online spaces and as pure mockery in others.

In the wild"Bro thinks he's alpha because he did one pull-up."

Dad translation"Top dog" / "pushover" — with a lot more baggage attached.

NoteHere's the fact worth having in your pocket: the "alpha wolf" idea comes from 1940s research on captive wolves, and the biologist who popularized it — David Mech — spent later decades trying to retract it. Wild wolf packs are family units; the "alpha" is just a parent. You are, technically, an alpha. It means dad.

🎓 What this module is actually teaching you

The vocabulary matters less than the value system it encodes: charisma is a stat, effort is a penalty, and self-promotion voids the score. If you understand that, you understand most of why your kids find certain adults excruciating. The adult who announces their own coolness has, in this framework, committed the cardinal sin. That's a useful thing to know before your next parent-chaperoned event.

03

Internet & Meme Culture — Where the Weird Words Come From

▼

This is the module where the words stop making etymological sense. "Skibidi" means nothing and everything. "Ohio" is an adjective. A guy named Fanum has a tax. If you've ever heard your son produce a full sentence in which every content word was a proper noun and thought I have had a stroke — this module is the antidote.

Skibidi

🟡 Once a month

MeansFunctionally: nothing fixed. It's a semantically empty intensifier — an adjective meaning silly, random, chaotic, bad, or good depending entirely on tone and context.

In the wild"This homework is skibidi." (bad) / "That was skibidi." (absurd, but delightful)

Dad translationThe closest millennial equivalent is "random" circa 2007 — a word that meant nothing but signalled which group you were in.

NoteFrom Skibidi Toilet, a YouTube animation series by DaFuq!?Boom! about singing heads in toilets fighting camera-headed people. Yes, really. It is Gen Alpha's defining media object and you will never understand it, which is by design.

Brainrot

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansTwo things at once: (1) the low-quality, hyper-stimulating content itself, and (2) the mental state of having consumed too much of it. Self-aware — kids describe themselves as having brainrot.

In the wild"I watched forty minutes of Subway Surfers gameplay under a Reddit story. Full brainrot."

Dad translation"Rotting my brain" / "doomscrolling" / your parents saying TV would ruin your eyes.

NoteOxford's Word of the Year 2024, and unusually useful for parents precisely because kids already agree it's bad. "That's brainrot" is a critique your children will accept from you, because they invented it.

NPC

non-player character🟢 Safe for dads

MeansSomeone acting robotic, scripted, or without independent thought — repeating the same lines, going through motions. From video games, where NPCs deliver the same dialogue on loop.

In the wild"He says 'living the dream' every single morning. Total NPC."

Dad translation"Going through the motions." / "Sheep." / "Autopilot."

NoteSafe for you because you actually have the gaming context. Careful though: calling a real person an NPC to their face is genuinely insulting — it's a claim they lack an inner life.

Ohio

🟡 Once a month

MeansWeird, cursed, off. Used as an adjective or a location for anything unhinged. "Only in Ohio" tags something that shouldn't exist.

In the wild"Why is there a raccoon in the car. This is so Ohio."

Dad translation"That's some Florida stuff." (Same joke, previous generation, different state.)

NoteThe state of Ohio has nothing to do with it — the meme started with random "only in Ohio" captions and self-propagated. If you have Ohio-based colleagues, they are aware, and they are tired.

Fanum tax

🟡 Once a month

MeansTaking a portion of someone's food — usually a friend's, usually without asking, usually as a bit. Extended to taking a cut of anything.

In the wildRehaan reaches over and takes three of Zara's fries: "Fanum tax."

Dad translation"Dad tax" — which you have been levying on their Halloween candy their entire lives without a name for it.

NoteNamed for streamer Fanum, who did this constantly on Kai Cenat's streams. This is your highest-value term in the entire course — you already do the behavior, so using the word is authentic rather than performed. Deploy at the dinner table.

Ratio

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansWhen a reply gets more likes than the post it's replying to — public evidence the crowd sided with the response. "You got ratioed" = you were publicly out-argued. Also used offline for any devastating comeback.

In the wild"Dad said the movie was too long and Zara said 'so is your story about the airport.' Ratio."

Dad translation"Owned." / "Roasted." / "He got dunked on."

NoteStarted as a literal metric on Twitter (reply count exceeding likes), then generalized. Now largely context-free — a kid can say "ratio" at a dinner table with no internet involved.

Touch grass

🟡 Once a month

MeansGo outside. Reconnect with physical reality. Said to someone too online, too invested in an argument, or too deep in a niche.

In the wild"You've been arguing about a video game economy for two hours. Touch grass."

Dad translation"Go outside and play." — which is the exact same sentence your parents said, now with a rhetorical edge.

NoteTempting for a parent and therefore dangerous. Coming from a dad it stops being a joke and becomes an instruction, which is precisely the transformation that makes it cringe. Use it about yourself instead: "I need to touch grass." That version lands.

Main character energy

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansBehaving like the protagonist of your own film — confident, self-directed, occasionally oblivious that others exist. Can be a compliment or a diagnosis.

In the wild"She walked into the party with her own playlist. Main character energy."

Dad translation"She owns the room." / "He thinks the world revolves around him." Both meanings, one phrase.

NoteSafe because the metaphor is fully transparent — nobody needs internet fluency to parse it. The related "NPC" (above) is the opposite pole of the same idea.

Side quest

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansA detour from the main plan — an unplanned errand, a random adventure, a tangent. From open-world games where side quests are optional missions off the main storyline.

In the wild"We were going to Target and ended up at the aquarium. Fun side quest."

Dad translation"A little detour." / "We took the scenic route."

NoteAnother green light with real utility — the gaming metaphor is legible to you, and it reframes an unplanned Saturday as a feature rather than a scheduling failure. Genuinely good for family logistics.

🍟 Field exercise

Next time you take food off one of your kids' plates — a behavior with a documented multi-decade history in your household — say "fanum tax" and keep eating. Say nothing else. Do not explain. Report results.

04

The Looks & Vibes Tier — Aura, Drip & the Mogging Economy

▼

Here's the module that starts funny and ends somewhere you should pay attention to. The first half is fashion vocabulary — harmless, useful, occasionally flattering to you. The second half is an appearance-optimization subculture that has real body-image consequences and has been documented as a pipeline into less friendly online spaces. You want to know both halves, and you want to know which is which.

Part A — Aura: social currency with a scoreboard

Aura

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansYour social presence, reputation, coolness — treated as a quantity you carry around. Someone can "have aura" or be "aura-less."

In the wild"He caught the ball one-handed without looking up. Pure aura."

Dad translation"Presence." / "He's got it." / Coolness as a noun.

NoteEffectively the successor to "rizz" as the umbrella term — broader, because aura covers all social impressiveness, not just romantic charm.

Aura points (+1000 / −500)

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansThe scoring system. Every action adds or subtracts aura, quoted in absurdly precise numbers for comic effect. Tripping in the hallway: −100 aura. Catching yourself mid-trip and continuing without acknowledging it: +500 aura.

In the wild"Dad parallel parked on the first try. Plus five hundred aura."

Dad translation"Cool points." Which was your generation's version, minus the precision.

NoteThis is the best parenting tool in the entire course. Awarding and deducting aura points is a low-stakes, funny, culturally native way to give feedback. "Unloading the dishwasher without being asked: plus a thousand aura" is a real sentence that will work in your house.

Drip

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansA stylish outfit; style in general. "Dripped out" = wearing something good. "No drip" = the fit is a failure.

In the wild"New sneakers? Okay, drip."

Dad translation"Swag" — a word you should now retire permanently, having read this sentence.

NoteHas hip-hop roots going back well before TikTok. Old enough now that it's stable rather than trendy, which is exactly what makes it safe for you.

Fit check

🟢 Safe for dads

Means"Show me your outfit" — a request or a self-initiated display. "Fit" = outfit.

In the wild"Fit check before we leave — are you seriously wearing that?"

Dad translation"Let's see what you're wearing."

NoteFully green. It does actual work in a household with school-morning arguments, and it's less confrontational than "you're changing before we go."

Serving

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansLooking exceptional. "Serving looks," "serving face," or just "she's serving." Sometimes "serving cunt" in its original ballroom form — which you should know exists so you're not blindsided by it.

In the wild"That photo? You were serving."

Dad translation"You look incredible."

NoteStraight out of ballroom and drag culture, where it's decades old. Comprehension is the goal here — a dad saying "you're serving" to his daughter's outfit is a documented cringe event.

Ate (and left no crumbs)

🟡 Once a month

MeansDid something flawlessly. The full form — "ate and left no crumbs" — means the performance was so complete there was nothing left to critique.

In the wild"Zara's recital solo? She ate. Left no crumbs."

Dad translation"Crushed it." / "Left nothing on the table."

NoteSame ballroom lineage as slay and serving. "She ate" alone is common and reasonably safe; the full "left no crumbs" is a bigger swing. Note it's the past tense of eat used as unqualified praise, which is why it sounds like nonsense the first time you hear it.

Part B — The optimization tier (read this part as a parent)

⚠️ Frame this correctly before you read on

The next three terms come from "looksmaxxing" communities. Among your kids' peers this is overwhelmingly a joke — boys do the mewing gesture in class the way you did the Macaulay Culkin face-slap. But the source communities are not a joke: they run on rating your face against numeric standards, and researchers and journalists have repeatedly documented them as an on-ramp to more extreme online spaces. Your job isn't to ban the word. It's to be able to tell which version is in the room.

Mewing

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansPressing your tongue against the roof of your mouth, supposedly to sharpen your jawline over time. In practice among kids: a gesture — finger to lips, then a finger traced along the jaw — meaning "shush, I'm mewing," used to refuse to answer a teacher.

In the wildTeacher: "Rehaan, what's the answer?" Rehaan: [finger to lips, traces jaw, says nothing.] Entire class loses it.

Dad translationNothing maps. The closest is sucking in your cheeks in a mirror, but with a whole methodology attached.

NoteNamed after orthodontist John Mew and his son Mike, whose "orthotropics" claims are not supported by mainstream orthodontics — Mike Mew was struck off the UK dental register in 2024. Among kids it is 95% a classroom bit. Knowing the gesture is the win here.

Looksmaxxing

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansSystematically optimizing your appearance. "Softmaxxing" = grooming, skincare, haircut, fitness — genuinely fine. "Hardmaxxing" = surgery, jaw implants, and considerably darker territory.

In the wild"He started looksmaxxing over the summer — new haircut, gym every day."

Dad translation"Getting in shape" / "cleaning himself up" — but with a rating system and a forum attached.

NoteThe -maxxing suffix is now completely general and mostly benign: "sleepmaxxing," "studymaxxing," "snackmaxxing." Hearing the suffix is not a warning sign. Hearing your kid talk about numeric facial ratings or "being an incel" is a different conversation.

Mogging / Frame mogging

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansMogging = visibly outshining someone by standing near them. Frame mogging = doing it specifically on physical build — being taller or broader so the comparison is unflattering. "He got mogged" = he was outclassed just by proximity.

In the wild"He stood next to his older brother in the photo and got absolutely frame mogged."

Dad translation"Showed him up." / "Made him look small." / Standing next to a groomsman who lifts.

NoteAlmost always jokey between friends. Same origin caveat as above. Your relevant fact: teenage boys are extremely aware of relative height and build right now in a way that has a vocabulary attached, and "mogged" is how it gets said out loud.

🎓 The parent read on Part B

Don't police the words — you'll lose, and you'll teach them to say it out of earshot. Watch instead for the shift from bit to belief: joking about mewing in the mirror is normal; talking seriously about facial ratios, "canthal tilt," or being genetically finished is the signal. The vocabulary is ambient. The sincerity is the thing to notice.

05

Emotional & Social Terms — The Relationship Vocabulary

▼

This module is the one you'll actually use as a parent — not for talking, for listening. Gen Z built a surprisingly precise vocabulary for relationship states that your generation had no words for at all. You dated someone or you didn't. They have gradations, taxonomies, and a red/green/beige classification system. Some of it is genuinely better than what you had.

Delulu

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansDelusional — usually about a crush, a chance, or an outcome. Almost always self-applied and affectionate rather than an insult. The catchphrase: "delulu is the solulu" (the delusion is the solution).

In the wild"I'm going to make varsity as a freshman. I know I'm delulu, let me have this."

Dad translation"Kidding myself." / "In my own head about it."

NoteGreen because it's self-deprecating by design, which is exactly the register that works for a dad. "I'm delulu about this fantasy lineup" is a legal sentence.

The ick

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansA sudden, irreversible turn-off — usually triggered by something tiny and irrational. You "catch the ick" or "get the ick," and once you have it, it doesn't leave.

In the wild"He ran for the bus and I caught the ick instantly."

Dad translation"Turned me off." / "Something about him just bugged me."

NoteThe key nuance most adults miss: the ick is understood to be unfair. Nobody defends it as rational. That's the joke — it's an involuntary reaction to someone chewing loudly or waving at their own reflection. It's fully green for you, and "ick" applies to non-romantic things too.

Beige flag

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansA trait that's neither a warning (red) nor a virtue (green) — just mildly odd. Neutral weirdness. Ordering the same meal every time. Narrating what they're doing while doing it.

In the wild"Dad says 'alrighty then' every time he stands up. Beige flag."

Dad translation"A quirk." / "That's just a him thing."

NoteA genuinely nice invention — it creates a middle category between "fine" and "problem" that your generation didn't have vocabulary for. You have many beige flags. You will be informed of them.

Red flag / Green flag

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansRed flag = a warning sign about someone's character. Green flag = evidence they're a good person. Applied to friends, dates, teachers, coaches, and jobs.

In the wild"He remembered her sister's name from one conversation. Green flag."

Dad translation"Warning sign" / "good sign." Red flag you already say. Green flag is the newer half.

NoteRed flag is decades old; green flag is the recent addition and it's the more interesting one — it made people name positive traits explicitly instead of only cataloguing problems. Note also that the flag framework gets over-applied online, where any minor preference gets escalated to a red flag.

Gaslighting

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansManipulating someone into doubting their own memory or perception. Properly: a sustained pattern, not a single disagreement.

In the wild"He told her the plan changed, then said she'd imagined the original plan. That's gaslighting."

Dad translation"Messing with your head." / "Making you feel crazy."

NoteFrom the 1938 play Gas Light and its film adaptations, where a husband dims the gaslights and insists his wife is imagining it. Worth knowing the real meaning because the term has been badly diluted online — kids use it for any disagreement. If your kid says a teacher gaslit them, the useful follow-up is "what did they say happened?", not a lecture on semantics.

Sneaky link

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansA secret hookup, or the person you're secretly meeting. Both noun and verb ("we're sneaky linking").

In the wildOverheard, never explained, and if you react you have revealed that you understood it.

Dad translation"Booty call" / "seeing someone on the down-low."

NoteStraightforwardly on the parental-awareness list. You want to recognize it in a group chat you're glancing at. You do not want to say it, ever, under any circumstances, including as a joke about meeting a colleague for coffee.

Situationship

🟡 Once a month

MeansA romantic connection that hasn't been defined — more than friends, not officially dating, deliberately or anxiously unlabelled.

In the wild"Are they together?" "It's a situationship."

Dad translation"They're talking." / "It's complicated." / "Undefined."

NoteLegitimately a useful word — it names a real, extremely common state with no prior label. Yellow only because a dad using it about his own life is a bit. You can absolutely use it about a TV show plot.

Pick me

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansSomeone who performs being different or agreeable to win approval — often by putting down their own peer group. "I'm not like other girls" is the canonical example.

In the wild"She said she'd rather hang out with guys because girls are drama. Pick me behavior."

Dad translation"Trying too hard to be liked." / "Attention-seeking" — though the original is more specific than either.

NoteIt's a real critique with a real target, and it's also frequently weaponized against girls for basically nothing. Know it so you can hear when it's being used unfairly about your daughter or by her. Red rating because it's an accusation, and dads issuing accusations in slang is a bad look.

Standing on business

🟡 Once a month

MeansBeing serious and following through — holding your position, handling what you said you'd handle. Sometimes "standing on bidness."

In the wild"I said no phone until homework's done and I'm standing on business."

Dad translation"Sticking to my guns." / "Handling my business." / "I said what I said."

NoteThis is the sleeper hit of the module for a parent, because the thing it describes is literally your job. Enforcing a boundary and naming it in their vocabulary is funnier and lands better than repeating the rule. Yellow rather than green only because overuse turns it into a catchphrase.

✅ The honest assessment of this module

Your generation had "it's complicated" and a shrug. Theirs has situationship, beige flag, the ick, and green flag — a genuinely finer-grained vocabulary for the middle states of relationships. That's not decline. That's a language doing its job. You can hold that thought and still refuse to say "sneaky link."

06

Reactions & Responses — What to Say When Something Happens

▼

Reactions are the easiest category to learn and the hardest to time. These are one-word responses to events, and the entire skill is delivery speed — a correct reaction two seconds late is worse than no reaction at all. Good news: several of these are already in your vocabulary from 2011, they just changed slightly. Bad news: one of them has two opposite meanings and you will get it wrong.

Dead / I'm dead / 💀

🟡 Once a month

MeansThat was hilarious. Also written as the skull emoji, which has almost entirely replaced 😂 among people under 25 — using the crying-laughing face now reads as distinctly middle-aged.

In the wild"He walked into the glass door twice. I'm dead 💀"

Dad translation"I'm dying." / "LOL" / literally 😂, the emoji you must now stop using.

NoteSwitching from 😂 to 💀 is the highest-leverage, lowest-effort change you can make in this entire course. It costs you nothing, it happens in text where nobody watches you do it, and it silently removes a marker.

Sheesh

"sheeeeeesh" — the length is the point🟡 Once a month

MeansAn impressed reaction — that's incredible / that's a lot. Drawn out for emphasis. Occasionally sarcastic.

In the wild"He dunked? Sheeeesh."

Dad translation"Daaaamn." / "Whoa."

NoteNote the shift: "sheesh" used to be an exasperated noise ("sheesh, calm down"). It flipped to admiration around 2021. If you use the old exasperated version your kids will hear the new one and be confused about why you're impressed that they left towels on the floor.

Sus

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansSuspicious, sketchy, not adding up. From Among Us, the 2020 game where you accuse crewmates of being the impostor.

In the wild"You're being very helpful today. Sus."

Dad translation"Sketchy." / "Something's off."

NoteOld enough now to be near-universal and completely safe. It also does real parenting work — "sus" is a much lighter way to flag that you've noticed something than "what are you not telling me."

Oof

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansThat was painful, awkward, or unfortunate — sympathetic or amused, depending on tone. From the Roblox death sound, which is why every kid under 14 knows it viscerally.

In the wild"You told her you liked her sweater and it was her mom's? Oof."

Dad translation"Ouch." / "Yikes."

NoteFully safe, near-universal, and requires zero performance — it's a noise, not a word. If you learn one reaction from this module, learn this one.

Naur

"NAW-uh"🔴 Do not attempt

Means"No," but dramatic — drawn out, theatrical, usually in mock distress. Written form of an exaggerated Australian pronunciation of "no."

In the wild"Naur, not the last samosa."

Dad translation"NOOOO." with your hands on your head.

NoteOriginated in Home and Away fan edits online. It's a performance word — it requires committing to a bit, and a half-committed "naur" from a dad is one of the more painful sounds available in nature.

Bro / Bruh

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansUniversal address and universal reaction. "Bruh" alone is a complete sentence expressing disbelief, disappointment, or resignation. "Bro" is also used in the third person to mock: "bro thinks he's funny."

In the wild"I got a C on it." "Bruh."

Dad translation"Dude." / "Man." / a long exhale.

NoteLearn the third-person mocking form — "bro really said that out loud" — because it's how your kids will describe you and you should know when it's happening. Gender-neutral in practice; girls say bro constantly.

Understood the assignment

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansDid exactly what the moment called for. Delivered precisely the right thing, without needing to be told.

In the wild"Everyone wore black to the party and Zara showed up in green. She understood the assignment."

Dad translation"Nailed the brief." / "Knew exactly what to do."

NoteThe most corporate-friendly item in this course — it's basically already business English, which is both why it's safe for you and why kids are slowly tiring of it.

Ate that

🟡 Once a month

MeansDid it excellently. Same family as "ate and left no crumbs" from Module 4 — "you ate that" is a direct compliment on a performance, outfit, or piece of work.

In the wild"Your presentation? You ate that."

Dad translation"You crushed it."

NoteCareful with word order. "You ate" = praise. "You ate that" = praise. "That ate" = praise. "Did you eat?" = a question about dinner and a completely different conversation.

Cooked

🟡 Once a month — read the note first

MeansFinished, doomed, destroyed. "He's cooked" = he's in serious trouble or has been thoroughly beaten. "We're cooked" = this is going badly.

In the wild"Test is tomorrow and I haven't started. I'm so cooked."

Dad translation"Toast." / "Done for." / "He's finished."

NoteThe trap: "let him cook" means the opposite — let him do his thing, he knows what he's doing. So "he's cooked" is a disaster and "let him cook" is a vote of confidence. Getting these backwards is the single most common dad error in this course. If Rehaan is on a roll, you say let him cook. If he tells you he's cooked, do not say "let him cook."

⚠️ The cooked/cooking trap, one more time

"He's cooked" → he is finished, in the bad way.
"Let him cook" → let him continue, he's doing something great.
"He's cooking" → he's currently doing something great.
Same verb, three states, two of them positive. Read the tense before you react.

07

2026 Breakout Slang — The Stuff That's Hot Right Now

▼

Everything above this point is reasonably stable. This module is the volatile tier — terms that broke through recently and are currently at peak usage. Half of these will feel dated by next summer. That's not a reason to skip it; it's the reason this is the module where comprehension matters most and production matters least. Also, the first term is about you.

Chopped unc

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansTwo words worth separating. Chopped = ugly, bad, ruined, low quality. Unc = short for uncle; an older guy, usually well-meaning, definitively out of touch. Together: an older man who is both clueless and trying.

In the wild"He tried to hit the griddy at the barbecue. Chopped unc behavior."

Dad translation"Sad dad." / "Trying way too hard." / What you were terrified of becoming, now with a name.

NoteImportant nuance: "unc" on its own is often affectionate. Kids call a cool older guy "unc" as a compliment — "unc status" can be earned. It's chopped unc that's the insult. This course is essentially a chopped-unc-prevention program, and the prevention method is understanding without performing.

Choppleganger

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansPortmanteau of "chopped" + "doppelgänger" — someone who looks like a worse version of someone else. The discount edition of a person.

In the wild"That guy at the store looked exactly like your coach but off. Total choppleganger."

Dad translation"Poor man's [name]." / "Diet [name]." / "Looks like [name] if he was drawn from memory."

NoteA genuinely clever construction and exactly the kind of term that burns bright and dies fast — high novelty, narrow use case. Enjoy it now; it likely won't survive the year.

Glazing

🟡 Once a month

MeansExcessive, embarrassing praise. Sucking up so hard it becomes uncomfortable to witness. "Stop glazing" = you're overdoing the compliments.

In the wild"He said his own team was the greatest of all time after one win. Insane glazing."

Dad translation"Kissing up." / "Riding someone's jock." / "Fanboying."

NoteCurrently one of the most-used words in this whole course among teenagers, and it's already crossing into adult usage — people accuse each other of glazing a product, a company, a model. Yellow rather than green mainly because saying it to a kid about their own hero is a hostile act.

Yapping

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansTalking too much, especially about nothing. A yapper is a person who does this. "Yap session" = a long, rambling conversation, sometimes fondly.

In the wild"Dad started explaining how the mortgage works again. He was yapping for twenty minutes."

Dad translation"Running your mouth." / "Going on and on."

NoteFully green, and the single most self-aware thing you can do in this course is preemptively call yourself a yapper. "Okay, I'm yapping, short version:" is a genuinely excellent sentence for a dad to own — it acknowledges the pattern before your kids have to.

Lock in

🟢 Safe for dads

MeansFocus. Get serious. Eliminate distractions and do the thing. "Locked in" = in a state of deep focus.

In the wild"Two weeks until finals. Time to lock in."

Dad translation"Buckle down." / "Get your head in the game." / "Focus up."

NoteThe most useful term in this entire module for a parent. It carries the exact motivational meaning you already want to communicate, it comes from sports and gaming rather than anything you'd have to explain, and it doesn't sound like nagging. This one is a genuine gift.

Edging

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansIn current kid usage: building suspense, delaying a payoff, stalling right before the good part. "He's edging us" = he keeps almost revealing the thing.

In the wild"He's been edging this announcement for three days."

Dad translation"Dragging it out." / "Blue-balling us" — which, notably, is the same joke your generation made with different words.

NoteYou should know that this word has a sexual origin, that most kids using it do so with limited awareness of that, and that this is precisely why it is on the never-say list for you. There is no version of a father saying "you're edging me" that ends well. Understand it silently and move on.

Mogging

🔴 Do not attempt

MeansOutshining someone by comparison — covered in Module 4, repeated here because 2026 usage has broadened well past looks. You can get mogged at anything: grades, sneakers, a fantasy draft.

In the wild"His science project had a working motor. Everyone else got mogged."

Dad translation"Showed everyone up." / "Blew them out of the water."

NoteThe broadening is the news here. When a term escapes its origin community and becomes generic, it usually means the original context has been forgotten — which is a normal, healthy stage of slang aging.

Tweaking

"tweakin'"🟡 Once a month

MeansActing irrationally, overreacting, saying something absurd. "You're tweaking" = you are being ridiculous right now.

In the wild"You want to leave at 6am for a 10am flight? You're tweaking."

Dad translation"You're trippin'." / "You're being crazy." — and "trippin'" is the exact structural ancestor.

NoteWorth knowing the older, harder meaning: "tweaking" has long referred to stimulant use. In current teen usage that connotation is essentially gone — it just means acting erratic. But it's why the word lands slightly harder than "trippin'."

🎓 Why this module has the most red ratings

Recency is the enemy of parental adoption. A term that broke through six months ago is still doing identity work — it marks who's current. Using it as a dad doesn't read as fluent, it reads as monitoring. The older terms in Modules 1 and 6 are safe precisely because they've stopped being markers. Rule of thumb: a word becomes safe for you roughly three years after it becomes popular. Which means your green list for 2029 is being written on TikTok right now, and there is nothing you can do about it.

The Parent Survival Guide

▼

The module with actual stakes. First: a small set of terms where the joke and the emergency use identical words, and how to tell them apart. Then: the practical rules for using any of this without becoming the thing this course is named after. Then a scenario quiz, and a note on why a third of what you just learned will be dead by next summer.

Part A — Terms that should get your attention

🚨 Read this framing first — it's the whole point of Part A

These terms are used as jokes constantly. A teenager typing "kms" because they got a B+ is not in crisis, and treating every instance as an emergency will teach your kids to stop typing anything in front of you — which is the exact opposite of what you want. The goal is not vigilance. The goal is calibration. You want to know the words so that when the pattern changes, you notice.

If you ever do need it: in the US, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), 24/7. Knowing the number in advance is the cheapest preparation available.

KMS / KYS

⚫ Know it, never say it

Means"Kill myself" / "kill yourself." In the overwhelming majority of teen usage these are hyperbole — "kms" for mild embarrassment, "kys" as a harsh but joking insult between friends.

In the wild"I said 'you too' to the waiter. kms." — this is a joke about awkwardness, not a signal.

Dad translation"I want to die" / "shoot me" — a phrase your generation used hyperbolically in exactly the same way.

NoteWhat actually changes the read: a shift from joking to flat and sincere; specificity instead of hyperbole; withdrawal, sleep changes, giving things away, or a sudden calm after a rough stretch. It's the pattern, not the acronym. If you're unsure, ask directly and plainly — asking about suicide does not plant the idea, and directness is what makes it answerable.

Unalive

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansA substitute for "kill" or "die," used to evade platform moderation. "Unalived himself," "unaliving."

In the wildUsed in true-crime and history videos as often as anywhere: "and then the general was unalived."

Dad translationNone. This is a genuinely new construction with no millennial equivalent.

NoteUnderstand where this comes from: algospeak — coded language invented to avoid automated moderation. Kids learned to route around content filters, and the vocabulary escaped into ordinary speech. Hearing "unalive" is usually a sign your kid watches a lot of short-form video, not a sign of distress. Same calibration principle as above: pattern, not word.

Plug

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansA supplier — the person who can get you something. Frequently drugs; also used innocuously for whoever gets you concert tickets, sneakers, or cheap textbooks.

In the wild"My cousin's the plug for the good samosas." (genuinely benign) vs. context you'd recognize immediately as not that.

Dad translation"My guy." / "A hookup." / "He knows a guy."

NoteNot old slang — "plug" has been in use for a long time and the innocuous version is extremely common. The word alone means little. The context around it means everything, which is a reason to stay curious rather than alarmed when you hear it.

Trap phone / burner

⚫ Know it, never say it

MeansA second, secret phone — one a parent doesn't know about. Historically drug-trade slang; among teens it's most often about evading parental monitoring generally.

In the wildAn old device that was supposed to be in a drawer, still charged, still on wifi.

Dad translation"A burner." / The 2005 equivalent: a second AIM screen name.

NotePractical note: the most common version isn't a purchased burner — it's an old phone that still works on wifi. If you retire a device in your house, wipe it or actually put it away. And the useful question if you find one isn't "how dare you," it's "what did you feel you couldn't do on the main phone?" The answer is the actual information.

✅ The one principle for Part A

Learn the words so you never have to ask what they mean. The value isn't in reacting to the vocabulary — it's that when you already know the words, you can spend your attention on tone, frequency, and change instead. A parent who has to stop and Google is a parent who has already lost the thread of the conversation.

Part B — How to use slang without being cringe

Six rules. They're ordered by how much damage ignoring them does.

Rule 1 — Never explain the slang to your kids

Say the word. Say nothing after the word. The instant you add "…which means agreed, right?" you've converted a moment of fluency into a vocabulary quiz, and you've signalled that you learned it deliberately. Deliberate is the tell. Casual is the whole trick.

Rule 2 — One term per conversation, maximum

Density is what makes it a performance. One "bet" in a twenty-minute conversation is a person talking. Four is a bit, and bits by definition require an audience, which makes it about you.

Rule 3 — Only use terms you'd use if nobody noticed

The test: would you say this word alone in your car? If the word only exists in your mouth when your kids are present, it isn't vocabulary, it's costume. "Lock in" passes this test. "Periodt" does not.

Rule 4 — Never correct their slang

You are not the authority here and claiming the authority is worse than being wrong. If your kid uses a word differently than this course describes, they're right and this course is out of date. That's how living language works. Update your model, not theirs.

Rule 5 — Never use it in front of their friends

In front of your kids, a well-placed "bet" is mildly amusing. In front of their friends, the same word costs your child social capital they have to spend the next week earning back. Home is the sandbox. Their friends are production.

Rule 6 — When you get it wrong, don't defend it

You will misuse something. The recovery is a shrug and moving on. Explaining why you thought it meant that is how a two-second miss becomes a story they tell for a decade. Take the L. That is, itself, correct usage.

Cringe version: "That's bussin, no cap! Am I saying that right? Bussin means good, right? Skibidi!"
— four terms, one explanation request, one term with no idea what it means. Total collapse.
Correct version: [tries the food] "Okay, that's bussin." [keeps eating, says nothing else]
— one term, zero explanation, no eye contact seeking approval. This survives.

Part D — Why a third of this will be dead by next summer

Slang has a lifecycle, and it's fast enough to observe in real time. Here's the pattern every one of these words follows:

StageWhat's happeningWho's using itYour status
1. OriginCoined in a specific community — often Black internet culture, ballroom, gaming, or a single streamerHundreds of peopleUnaware
2. SpreadPicked up by short-form video, repeated in edits and comment sectionsTeens, earlyUnaware
3. PeakEveryone under 20 uses it; it marks who's currentAll teensYou first hear it, confused
4. SaturationBrands use it. A cereal account tweets it. Teachers say it.EveryoneYou learn it — you are here
5. Ironic useKids use it only as a joke, specifically because adults adopted itTeens, mockinglyYou use it sincerely. Fatal.
6. CheugyDead. Using it straight now marks you as behind.Nobody, or parentsRetire it

Cheugy itself is the perfect demonstration: it was coined to describe things that were out of date, went viral in 2021, got adopted by every brand and news outlet within months, and is now — with genuine irony — itself cheugy. The word for outdated became outdated. Nothing illustrates the lifecycle better.

📉 Your generation's graveyard, for calibration

On fleek. Bae. Yaaas. Adulting. Totes. Cray. Squad goals. YOLO. Turnt. "I can't even." Every one of those was, briefly, the correct thing to say. You have already lived through a full cycle — that's why you can feel how badly "on fleek" ages. Your kids will feel exactly this way about "skibidi" in about four years, and they will be equally embarrassed. The cycle is the constant. The words are just the payload.

✅ The takeaway that outlasts every word in this course

The vocabulary is disposable. What isn't disposable is the habit of staying curious rather than dismissive. Every generation's parents decided the new words meant the language was in decline, and every generation was wrong. Your kids are doing something linguistically ordinary and completely normal. Understanding it — not performing it — is the whole assignment. And you, unlike most parents, understood the assignment.
